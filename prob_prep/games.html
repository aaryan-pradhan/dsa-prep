0 · Ground rules

  1. Setting: two players alternate, with the same moves available to both. The player with no legal move loses. The game is finite, so there are no draws.
  2. L-position (losing for the player to move): every move goes to a W-position. A position with no moves is L.
  3. W-position: some move goes to an L-position.
  4. In a finite game every position is exactly one of W or L. (induction backwards from the end)

So to prove a player wins, give them a way to always move into L.

1 · Symmetry

Make the position symmetric (you move first) or keep it symmetric (you move second). Then copy the opponent's move on the mirror image. If they had a move, you have one too, so you are never the one stuck.

Removing petals

A flower has 11 petals in a circle. A move tears off 1 petal, or 2 adjacent petals. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Alice's first move turns the circle into a line. Bob cuts that line into two equal halves and then mirrors Alice.

Solution

Setup. 11 petals in a circle. A move removes 1 petal or 2 adjacent petals (neighbours with no gap between them). The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first.

Claim. Bob wins.

  1. After Alice's first move, the remaining petals form a single line of 10 (if she took 1) or 9 (if she took 2).Why: removing petals from a circle breaks it at that spot; the petals on either side of a gap are not adjacent.
  2. Bob removes the middle 2 petals of the line of 10, or the middle 1 petal of the line of 9. Either way, two separate lines of 4 petals remain.Why: \(10 - 2 = 8 = 4 + 4\) and \(9 - 1 = 8 = 4 + 4\), with the gap in the middle.
  3. From then on, pair petal \(j\) of one line with petal \(j\) of the other. Whatever Alice removes from one line (1 petal or 2 adjacent ones), Bob removes the matching petals from the other line.Why: this is Bob's strategy.
  4. Bob's reply is always legal, and after it the two lines are identical again.Why: before Alice's move the lines were identical, so the matching petals are present and adjacent in the other line. No move can use petals from both lines, because they are not adjacent.
  5. The game is finite and Bob always has a reply, so Alice is the one who gets stuck.Why: each move removes at least one petal.

Conclusion. Bob splits the line into two equal halves and mirrors Alice. ∎

⚠ Course says “Bob can always leave two identical rows” but not how. Step 2 is the how.

Chords

20 points on a circle. A move draws a segment between two of the points that does not cross any segment already drawn. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Alice draws a "diameter" with 9 points on each side, then answers every move with its mirror image across that diameter.

Solution

Setup. 20 points on a circle. A move draws a segment between two points that crosses no segment already drawn (sharing an endpoint is allowed). The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first.

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. Only the cyclic order of the points matters, so place them evenly. Alice joins two opposite points \(D_1, D_2\), leaving 9 points on each side. Let \(\sigma\) be the reflection in the line \(D_1D_2\); it swaps the two sides.Why: crossing depends only on the order of endpoints around the circle.
  2. Alice's rule: when Bob draws a chord \(c\), she draws \(\sigma(c)\). Invariant: after each of Alice's moves, the drawing is symmetric under \(\sigma\).Why: true after her first move, and each reply adds the mirror of Bob's chord.
  3. Bob's chord \(c\) can't cross \(D_1D_2\), so it lies in one closed half, and \(\sigma(c)\) lies in the other.Why: \(D_1D_2\) is already drawn.
  4. \(\sigma(c)\) is a legal move. It is not already drawn: otherwise \(c = \sigma(\sigma(c))\) was drawn too, by the invariant. It doesn't cross \(c\): they lie in opposite halves and can meet only on \(D_1D_2\), at shared endpoints. It doesn't cross an older chord \(e\): otherwise \(c\) would cross \(\sigma(e)\), which is also drawn by the invariant.Why: reflection preserves crossings.
  5. So Alice always has a move. There are finitely many chords, so Bob gets stuck.Why: steps 2–4.

Conclusion. Alice draws a diameter and mirrors Bob across it. ∎

⚠ Course says “reply symmetrically” without naming the symmetry (reflection in the first chord) or checking that the reply is legal (step 4).

2 · Strategy stealing

Proves that the first player wins without finding the strategy. Find a "waiting" move \(w\) that the opponent can never use against you.

  1. If \(w\) leads to an L-position, play \(w\).
  2. Otherwise the opponent has a winning reply \(x\) after \(w\). Show that you could have reached the same position by playing \(x\) yourself first. Then play \(x\).

Needs a finite game with no draws.

Game of Reduction

Distinct positive integers \(\{1000, 2000, 2024\}\). A move replaces the largest number with a smaller positive integer, keeping all numbers distinct. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

\(2024 \to 2001\) is a waiting move: Bob then has to replace 2001 with some \(v\), and Alice could have replaced 2024 with \(v\) directly.

Solution

Setup. The numbers \(\{1000, 2000, 2024\}\). A move replaces the largest number with a smaller positive integer, keeping all three numbers distinct. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first.

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. The sum strictly decreases, so the game is finite, and every position is W or L.Why: ground rules.
  2. For \(v \in \{1, \dots, 1999\}\), \(v \ne 1000\), let \(S(v) = \{1000, 2000, v\}\). Alice can reach any \(S(v)\) directly by moving \(2024 \to v\).Why: \(v < 2024\) and distinct from 1000 and 2000.
  3. Case A: some \(S(v)\) is L. Alice moves there, Bob faces an L-position, and Alice wins.Why: definition of L.
  4. Case B: every \(S(v)\) is W. Alice moves \(2024 \to 2001\) (legal). Now the largest number is 2001, and Bob must replace it with some \(v < 2001\), \(v \notin \{1000, 2000\}\), i.e. \(v \le 1999\). That position is \(S(v)\), which is W for the next player, Alice.Why: Bob has no other kind of move.
  5. In both cases Alice wins.Why: the two cases cover everything.

Conclusion. The waiting move \(2024 \to 2001\) steals any winning reply from Bob. ∎

Game of primes

Alice says 2. Then players alternate naming primes. From \(n\), where \(p\) is the next prime after \(n\), you may say any prime \(m\) with \(n < m \le n + 2(p - n)\). Whoever says 4567 (a prime) wins. Who wins?

Key idea

Bob is forced to say 3. From 3, Alice can say 5 or 7, and saying 5 forces Bob to say 7. So Alice chooses who says 7.

Solution

Setup. Alice says 2. From the last number \(n\), with \(p\) the next prime after \(n\), the next player may say any prime \(m\) with \(n < m \le n + 2(p - n)\). Whoever says 4567 (a prime) wins.

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. Jumping past 4567 is never better than saying 4567: whenever a jump past it is legal, the allowed range is an interval that contains \(p \le 4567\) and a number above 4567, so it contains 4567 itself. Treat jumping as forbidden; then the game is finite and every position is W or L.Why: the allowed range is an interval starting just above \(n\).
  2. From 2: \(p = 3\), so the range is \(\le 2 + 2 = 4\), and the only prime is 3. Bob must say 3.Why: the rule.
  3. From 3: \(p = 5\), so the range is \(\le 3 + 4 = 7\). Alice may say 5 or 7.Why: the primes in \((3, 7]\).
  4. From 5: \(p = 7\), so the range is \(\le 5 + 4 = 9\), and the only prime is 7. The reply to 5 must be 7.Why: the primes in \((5, 9]\).
  5. The position right after someone says 7 is either W or L for the next player. If it is L, saying 7 is winning, and Alice says 7. If it is W, Alice says 5, and Bob is forced to say 7, handing Alice a W-position.Why: steps 3 and 4.

Conclusion. Alice controls who says 7, so she wins. (Checked by brute force.) ∎

⚠ Course ignores the possibility of jumping past 4567 (the game could run forever, a draw). Step 1 closes that gap.

Divisors III

\(1,\dots,2025\) are on a board. A move erases an available number together with all its not-yet-erased divisors. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Erasing just 1 is the waiting move, because 1 divides everything.

Solution

Setup. The numbers 1–2025 are on the board. A move erases an available number together with all its divisors that are still on the board. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first.

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. The game is finite with no draws, so every position is W or L.Why: each move erases at least one number.
  2. If the position after erasing just 1 is L, Alice erases 1 and wins.Why: she hands Bob an L-position.
  3. Otherwise that position is W, so Bob has a winning reply there: erase some \(x > 1\), reaching an L-position. That position has erased exactly 1 and the divisors of \(x\).Why: definition of W.
  4. Alice can reach the same position in one move by erasing \(x\) herself: that erases \(x\) and all its divisors, including 1.Why: \(1 \mid x\).
  5. Either way Alice moves into an L-position and wins.Why: steps 2–4.

Conclusion. Erasing 1 is a waiting move that Bob can never use against Alice. ∎

3 · Winning / losing states

Guess the L-positions from small cases, then prove two things: (i) every move from L goes to W; (ii) from every W there is a move to L.

The small cases come from a rec:

  1. Level: position \(n\); win(n) = the player to move wins.
  2. Choice: every legal move \(n \to m\).
  3. Check: no legal move → lose.
  4. Move: win(n) = some move reaches a losing \(m\), i.e. \(\exists\, m: \neg\text{win}(m)\).

300 marbles

A move removes \(r\) marbles, with \(1 \le r \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\). The player who can't move loses. Start with 300. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

The L-positions are \(n = 2^k - 1\). Found by printing the rec:

bool win(ll n) {
    for (ll r = 1; r <= n / 2; r++)
        if (!win(n - r)) return true;
    return false;
}   // !win(n) for n = 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, ...
Solution

Setup. From \(n\) marbles a move removes \(r\) with \(1 \le r \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\). The player who can't move loses. Start at 300; Alice moves first. Claim: the L-positions are exactly \(n = 2^k - 1\).

Claim. Alice wins (take 45, leaving 255).

  1. \(n = 1 = 2^1 - 1\): no move is possible, so it is L.Why: \(\lfloor 1/2 \rfloor = 0\).
  2. From \(n = 2^k - 1\), every move leads to a non-L position.Why: \(r \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor = 2^{k-1} - 1\), so what's left is between \(2^k - 1 - (2^{k-1} - 1) = 2^{k-1}\) and \(2^k - 2\). No number in that range has the form \(2^j - 1\): it lies strictly between \(2^{k-1} - 1\) and \(2^k - 1\).
  3. From any other \(n \ge 2\), there is a move to an L-position.Why: pick \(k\) with \(2^{k-1} \le n \le 2^k - 2\). Remove \(r = n - (2^{k-1} - 1) \ge 1\). This is allowed because \(r \le n/2 \iff n \le 2^k - 2\). It leaves \(2^{k-1} - 1\).
  4. So \(L = \{2^k - 1\}\).Why: steps 1–3 are exactly the two properties that define L and W.
  5. \(300\) is not of that form; \(256 \le 300 \le 510\) gives \(k = 9\), and Alice removes \(300 - 255 = 45\), leaving \(255 = 2^8 - 1\).Why: step 3.

Conclusion. Alice moves to 255 and then always returns Bob to the next \(2^k - 1\). ∎

⚠ Course text has wrong bounds (“between \(2^k-1\) and \(2^{k-2}\)”) and doesn't check \(r \le n/2\) (step 3).

Divisors II

60 is on the board. A move replaces \(n\) with \(n - d\), where \(d \mid n\). Whoever writes 0 loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Odd numbers are L. An odd number has only odd divisors, and from an even number you subtract 1.

Solution

Setup. 60 is on the board. A move replaces \(n\) with \(n - d\) for a divisor \(d\) of \(n\). Whoever writes 0 loses. Alice moves first.

Claim. Alice wins (\(60 \to 59\)).

  1. Claim: for odd \(n\), the player to move loses. Proof by strong induction on \(n\).Why: guessed from small cases.
  2. \(n = 1\): the only divisor is 1, so the move writes 0 and loses.Why: no other choice.
  3. Odd \(n > 1\): every divisor \(d\) is odd, so \(n - d\) is even. If \(n - d = 0\), the mover loses at once. Otherwise \(n - d \ge 2\) is even, and the opponent writes \(n - d - 1\) (subtracting the divisor 1): an odd number, at least 1 and less than \(n\). By the hypothesis, the original mover now loses from it.Why: odd numbers have only odd divisors, and odd minus odd is even.
  4. 60 is even. Alice writes \(60 - 1 = 59\), which is odd, so Bob, to move, loses.Why: step 3 with \(n = 59\).

Conclusion. Alice keeps handing Bob odd numbers. ∎

4 · Endgame analysis

Work backwards from the final position and label each position W or L. Then read off the pattern and prove it as in §3.

Moving king

\(8\times8\) board, king in the bottom-left corner. A move goes one step up, one step right, or one step diagonally up-right. Whoever reaches the top-right corner wins. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Measure \((a, b)\) = the distance still to go. The L-positions are those with \(a\) and \(b\) both even.

Solution

Setup. Let \((a, b)\) be the distance the king still has to go up and right; the start is \((7, 7)\). A move goes to \((a-1, b)\), \((a, b-1)\) or \((a-1, b-1)\), staying \(\ge 0\). Whoever reaches \((0,0)\) wins, so \((0,0)\) is an L-position for the player to move.

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. From \((\text{even}, \text{even}) \ne (0, 0)\), every move makes \(a\) or \(b\) odd.Why: each move subtracts 1 from \(a\), from \(b\), or from both.
  2. From a position that is not (even, even): if both are odd, move diagonally; if only one is odd, reduce that one. Either way you reach (even, even).Why: subtracting 1 flips parity.
  3. So the L-positions are exactly (even, even).Why: steps 1–2 plus the base \((0,0)\).
  4. The start \((7, 7)\) is not (even, even), so it is W: Alice moves to \((6, 6)\) and keeps returning to (even, even).Why: step 2.

Conclusion. Alice moves diagonally to \((6,6)\) and always restores even–even. ∎

⚠ Course shows only the board picture (above). Steps 1–2 are the proof behind it.

5 · Ad-hoc

Look for a quantity fixed by the rules (so the total number of moves is decided in advance), or a pairing/colouring from the brainteasers toolkit.

Three piles

Piles of 10, 15 and 20 stones. A move splits one pile of \(\ge 2\) stones into two non-empty piles. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Each move adds exactly one pile. So the number of moves is fixed.

Solution

Setup. Piles of 10, 15 and 20 stones. A move splits one pile of at least 2 stones into two non-empty piles. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first.

Claim. Bob wins.

  1. Each move adds exactly 1 pile.Why: one pile becomes two.
  2. The game ends only when every pile has 1 stone, i.e. when there are \(10 + 15 + 20 = 45\) piles.Why: a pile of \(\ge 2\) can still be split.
  3. So exactly \(45 - 3 = 42\) moves are played, whatever anyone does.Why: steps 1–2.
  4. 42 is even, so the last move is Bob's, and Alice is then stuck.Why: moves alternate starting with Alice.

Conclusion. The number of moves is fixed at 42, so Bob moves last. ∎

Moving the piece

\(183\times181\) grid. A piece starts top-left. A move goes to an adjacent unvisited cell (diagonals count). The start cell counts as visited. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

\(mn\) is odd, so the cells other than the start can be split into adjacent pairs. Bob always moves to the partner of Alice's cell.

Solution

Setup. A \(183\times181\) grid (183 rows, 181 columns). The piece starts in the top-left cell, which counts as visited. A move goes to an adjacent unvisited cell (diagonals count). The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first.

Claim. Bob wins.

  1. Pair up all cells except the start: the top row without the corner has 180 cells, paired horizontally; the other 182 rows are paired vertically, column by column (each column has 182 cells there). Partners are adjacent. Same shape on \(5\times5\):Why: 180 and 182 are even, so everything pairs up.
  2. Bob's rule: whenever Alice moves into a cell, Bob moves into its partner.Why: this is the strategy.
  3. Invariant: after each of Bob's moves, every pair is either fully visited or fully unvisited.Why: true at the start (the start cell is in no pair), and each round visits exactly one whole pair.
  4. So Alice always steps into an untouched pair. Its partner is adjacent and unvisited, so Bob can move there and keep the invariant.Why: step 3.
  5. Bob always has a move, and the cells run out, so Alice is stuck.Why: finite board.

Conclusion. The pairing lets Bob answer every move. ∎

⚠ Course shows only a coloured \(5\times5\) picture. Step 1 is the explicit pairing for any odd \(m, n\).
