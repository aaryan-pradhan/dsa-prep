0 · Ground rules

  1. Setting: two players alternate, with the same moves available to both. The player with no legal move loses. The game is finite, so there are no draws.
  2. L-position (losing for the player to move): every move goes to a W-position. A position with no moves is L.
  3. W-position: some move goes to an L-position.
  4. In a finite game every position is exactly one of W or L. (induction backwards from the end)

So to prove a player wins, give them a way to always move into L.

1 · Symmetry

Make the position symmetric (you move first) or keep it symmetric (you move second). Then copy the opponent's move on the mirror image. If they had a move, you have one too, so you are never the one stuck.

Removing petals

A flower has 11 petals in a circle. A move tears off 1 petal, or 2 adjacent petals. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Alice's first move turns the circle into a line. Bob cuts that line into two equal halves and then mirrors Alice.

Solution

Claim. Bob wins.

  1. After Alice's first move, a line of 10 or 9 petals remains. (\(11 - 1\) or \(11 - 2\))
  2. Bob removes the middle 2 (from 10) or the middle 1 (from 9). That leaves two separate lines of 4 petals each.
  3. From then on, whatever Alice removes from one line, Bob removes the same positions from the other. The two lines stay identical after each of Bob's moves.
  4. So Bob always has a reply. Petals run out, so Alice is the one who gets stuck.

∎

⚠ Course says "Bob can always leave two identical rows" but not how. Step 2 is the how.

Chords

20 points on a circle. A move draws a segment between two of the points that does not cross any segment already drawn. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Alice draws a "diameter" with 9 points on each side, then answers every move with its mirror image across that diameter.

Solution

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. Only the cyclic order of the points matters, so place them evenly. Alice joins two opposite points, \(D_1\) and \(D_2\). That leaves 9 points on each side. Let \(\sigma\) be the reflection in line \(D_1D_2\).
  2. Invariant: after each of Alice's moves, the drawing is mapped to itself by \(\sigma\). (true after 1)
  3. Bob draws a legal chord \(c\). No chord crosses \(D_1D_2\), so \(c\) lies in one closed half and \(\sigma(c)\) lies in the other.
  4. \(\sigma(c)\) is legal. It is not already drawn: if it were, \(c\) would be too, by 2. It does not cross \(c\): the two meet only on \(D_1D_2\), and at most at shared endpoints. It does not cross any older chord \(e\): if it did, \(c\) would cross \(\sigma(e)\), which is also drawn by 2.
  5. So Alice always has a move. There are finitely many chords, so Bob gets stuck.

∎

⚠ Course says "reply symmetrically" without naming the symmetry (reflection in the first chord) or checking that the reply is legal (step 4).

2 · Strategy stealing

Proves that the first player wins without finding the strategy. Find a "waiting" move \(w\) that the opponent can never use against you.

  1. If \(w\) leads to an L-position, play \(w\).
  2. Otherwise the opponent has a winning reply \(x\) after \(w\). Show that you could have reached the same position by playing \(x\) yourself first. Then play \(x\).

Needs a finite game with no draws.

Game of Reduction

Distinct positive integers \(\{1000, 2000, 2024\}\). A move replaces the largest number with a smaller positive integer, keeping all numbers distinct. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

\(2024 \to 2001\) is a waiting move: Bob then has to replace 2001 with some \(v\), and Alice could have replaced 2024 with \(v\) directly.

Solution

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. The sum strictly decreases, so the game is finite and each position is W or L. (ground rules)
  2. For \(v \in \{1,\dots,1999\} \setminus \{1000\}\), let \(S(v)\) be the position \(\{1000, 2000, v\}\). Alice can move \(2024 \to v\) and reach any \(S(v)\).
  3. Case A: some \(S(v)\) is L. Alice moves there and wins.
  4. Case B: every \(S(v)\) is W. Alice moves \(2024 \to 2001\). Bob must replace 2001 with some \(v < 2001\), \(v \notin \{1000, 2000\}\), so he reaches an \(S(v)\), which is W for Alice, the next player to move.
  5. Either way Alice moves into an L-position.

∎

Game of primes

Alice says 2. Then players alternate naming primes. From \(n\), where \(p\) is the next prime after \(n\), you may say any prime \(m\) with \(n < m \le n + 2(p - n)\). Whoever says 4567 (a prime) wins. Who wins?

Key idea

Bob is forced to say 3. From 3, Alice can say 5 or 7, and saying 5 forces Bob to say 7. So Alice chooses who says 7.

Solution

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. If jumping past 4567 is legal, then saying 4567 is also legal, because the allowed range is an interval containing \(p \le 4567\). So jumping is never a better move. Treat it as forbidden: the game is finite, and every position is W or L.
  2. From 2: the range is \(\le 2 + 2\cdot 1 = 4\), so Bob must say 3.
  3. From 3: the range is \(\le 3 + 4 = 7\), so Alice may say 5 or 7.
  4. From 5: the range is \(\le 5 + 4 = 9\), and the only prime there is 7, so the reply must be 7.
  5. If saying 7 wins (the position after 7 is L for the next player), Alice says 7. Otherwise she says 5 and Bob is forced to say 7. (3, 4)

∎

⚠ Course ignores the possibility of jumping past 4567 (the game could run forever, a draw). Step 1 closes that gap. Checked by brute force.

Divisors III

\(1,\dots,2025\) are on a board. A move erases an available number together with all its not-yet-erased divisors. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Erasing just 1 is the waiting move, because 1 divides everything.

Solution

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. The game is finite with no draws.
  2. If the position after erasing only 1 is L, Alice erases 1 and wins.
  3. Otherwise Bob has a winning reply \(x\) (\(x > 1\)). The result is \(\{1\} \cup \{\text{divisors of } x\}\) erased, which is the same as erasing \(x\) first, because \(1 \mid x\).
  4. So Alice erases \(x\) on move 1 and reaches that same L-position.

∎

3 · Winning / losing states

Guess the L-positions from small cases, then prove two things: (i) every move from L goes to W; (ii) from every W there is a move to L.

300 marbles

A move removes \(r\) marbles, with \(1 \le r \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\). The player who can't move loses. Start with 300. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

The L-positions are \(n = 2^k - 1\).

Solution

Claim. Alice wins (take 45, leaving 255).

  1. \(n = 1 = 2^1 - 1\): no move, so L.
  2. From \(n = 2^k - 1\): \(r \le 2^{k-1} - 1\), so what's left is in \([2^{k-1},\, 2^k - 2]\). No number there has the form \(2^j - 1\). (it lies strictly between \(2^{k-1}-1\) and \(2^k-1\))
  3. From \(n \in [2^{k-1},\, 2^k - 2]\): take \(r = n - (2^{k-1} - 1) \ge 1\). We need \(r \le n/2 \iff n \le 2^k - 2\). ✓ This leaves \(2^{k-1} - 1\).
  4. So \(L = \{2^k - 1\}\). 300 is not of that form, and \(300 - 45 = 255 = 2^8 - 1\).

∎

⚠ Course text has wrong bounds ("between \(2^k-1\) and \(2^{k-2}\)") and doesn't check \(r \le n/2\) (step 3).

Divisors II

60 is on the board. A move replaces \(n\) with \(n - d\), where \(d \mid n\). Whoever writes 0 loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Odd numbers are L. An odd number has only odd divisors, and from an even number you subtract 1.

Solution

Claim. Alice wins (\(60 \to 59\)).

  1. Claim: an odd \(n\) is L. Proof by strong induction on \(n\).
  2. \(n = 1\): the only move writes 0, so you lose.
  3. Odd \(n > 1\): every divisor \(d\) is odd, so \(n - d\) is even. If it is 0 you lose. Otherwise the opponent writes \(n - d - 1\), which is odd, \(\ge 1\) and less than \(n\), so it is L by hypothesis.
  4. 60 is even. Alice writes 59, which is L for Bob.

∎

4 · Endgame analysis

Work backwards from the final position and label each position W or L. Then read off the pattern and prove it as in §3.

Moving king

\(8\times8\) board, king in the bottom-left corner. A move goes one step up, one step right, or one step diagonally up-right. Whoever reaches the top-right corner wins. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Measure \((a, b)\) = the distance still to go. The L-positions are those with \(a\) and \(b\) both even.

Solution

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. A move takes \((a,b)\) to \((a-1,b)\), \((a,b-1)\) or \((a-1,b-1)\). Reaching \((0,0)\) wins, so for the player to move, \((0,0)\) is L.
  2. From \((\text{even}, \text{even}) \ne (0,0)\), every move makes \(a\) or \(b\) odd.
  3. From a position that is not (even, even): if both are odd, move diagonally; if only one is odd, reduce that one. Either way you reach (even, even).
  4. So \(L = \{(\text{even}, \text{even})\}\). The start is \((7,7)\), so it is W.

∎

⚠ Course shows only coloured board pictures. Steps 2–3 are the proof behind them.

5 · Ad-hoc

Look for a quantity fixed by the rules (so the total number of moves is decided in advance), or a pairing/colouring from the brainteasers toolkit.

Three piles

Piles of 10, 15 and 20 stones. A move splits one pile of \(\ge 2\) stones into two non-empty piles. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Each move adds exactly one pile. So the number of moves is fixed.

Solution

Claim. Bob wins.

  1. Each move adds exactly 1 pile.
  2. The game ends only when every pile has 1 stone, which is 45 piles.
  3. So there are exactly \(45 - 3 = 42\) moves, whatever anyone does. (1, 2)
  4. 42 is even, so the last move is Bob's.

∎

Moving the piece

\(183\times181\) grid. A piece starts top-left. A move goes to an adjacent unvisited cell (diagonals count). The start cell counts as visited. The player who can't move loses. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

\(mn\) is odd, so the cells other than the start can be split into adjacent pairs. Bob always moves to the partner of Alice's cell.

Solution

Claim. Bob wins.

  1. Pairing: top row without the corner, 180 cells (even), in horizontal pairs. The remaining 182 rows (even) in vertical pairs, column by column.
  2. Invariant: after each of Bob's moves, every pair is either fully visited or fully unvisited.
  3. Alice therefore steps into an untouched pair. Its partner is adjacent and unvisited, so Bob moves there and keeps 2.
  4. So Bob always has a move. The cells run out, so Alice is stuck.

∎

⚠ Course shows only a coloured \(5\times5\) picture. Step 1 is the explicit pairing for any odd \(m, n\).
