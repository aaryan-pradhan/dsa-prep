0 · How to practise

  1. Effort, not outcome. Solving the problem is not the goal; trying hard is what improves you. Remove distractions.
  2. Switch approach when one stalls, even if it gave "progress".
  3. Solutions come from experiments (often in the wrong direction) plus noticing things.
  4. Stuck? One hint at a time. After the solution ask: how could I have found this? why didn't I?
  5. Level: practise just above your comfort level; move up when it gets comfortable.

1 · Small, special, random cases

Solve a smaller version first (fewer objects, lower dimension, fewer constraints). Then try special cases (e.g. equal piles), then random ones. The pattern in the small case usually works in general.

Lighter box

10 boxes of 100 coins each. 9 boxes have 10 g coins, 1 box has 9 g coins. Find the light box with one weighing (the scale shows the total weight).

Key idea

Take a different number of coins from each box, so every possible light box gives a different total.

Solution

Setup. The boxes are numbered \(1, 2, \dots, 10\). A normal coin weighs 10 g and a light coin weighs 9 g. Exactly one box is light; call its number \(j\). We may use the scale once, and it shows the total weight \(W\) of whatever we put on it.

Claim. Put \(k\) coins from box \(k\) on the scale, for every \(k\). Then the light box is \(j = 550 - W\).

  1. Take 1 coin from box 1, 2 coins from box 2, …, 10 coins from box 10, and weigh all of them together.Why: each box has 100 coins, so we can always take \(k \le 10\) of them. Taking a different number from each box is what will make each possible light box give a different reading.
  2. The scale holds \(1 + 2 + \dots + 10 = 55\) coins.Why: \(1 + 2 + \dots + n = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\), and \(\tfrac{10 \cdot 11}{2} = 55\).
  3. If every coin weighed 10 g, the reading would be \(55 \cdot 10 = 550\) g.Why: 55 coins (step 2), 10 g each.
  4. In reality the \(j\) coins taken from box \(j\) each weigh 1 g less, and every other coin weighs exactly 10 g. So \(W = 550 - j\).Why: only box \(j\) is light, and we took exactly \(j\) coins from it (step 1). Each of those coins removes 1 g from the 550 g of step 3.
  5. Rearranging step 4: \(j = 550 - W\).Why: add \(j\) to both sides of \(W = 550 - j\) and subtract \(W\).
  6. The ten possible light boxes give ten different readings: \(W = 549, 548, \dots, 540\) for \(j = 1, 2, \dots, 10\). So one reading always tells us \(j\).Why: \(W = 550 - j\) is different for different \(j\).

Conclusion. Weigh \(k\) coins from box \(k\) for all \(k\) and read the light box off as \(j = 550 - W\). ∎

Two piles of stones

Piles of 1933 and 3800 stones. A move removes \(\ge 1\) stones from one pile. The player who takes the last stone wins. Alice moves first. Who wins?

Key idea

Special case: with equal piles the second player wins by copying the first player's move on the other pile.

Solution

Setup. Pile A has 1933 stones and pile B has 3800. A move chooses one pile and removes at least 1 stone from it. Whoever takes the last stone wins. Alice moves first.

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. Alice's first move: remove \(3800 - 1933 = 1867\) stones from pile B. Now both piles have 1933 stones.Why: 1867 is at least 1 and at most 3800, so this is a legal move.
  2. Alice's rule for every later move: if Bob just removed \(t\) stones from one pile, Alice removes \(t\) stones from the other pile.Why: this is the strategy we will check.
  3. After every one of Alice's moves the two piles are equal.Why: it is true after step 1. Suppose the piles are equal, \((x, x)\), and Bob removes \(t\) from one of them, giving \((x - t, x)\). Alice removes \(t\) from the other, giving \((x - t, x - t)\), which is equal again.
  4. Alice's copying move is always legal.Why: Bob removed \(t\) stones from a pile of size \(x\), so \(t \le x\). By step 3 the other pile also has \(x\) stones, so Alice can remove \(t\) from it.
  5. Bob can never take the last stone.Why: before Bob moves the piles are \((x, x)\) (step 3) with \(x \ge 1\). Bob changes only one pile, so the other pile still has \(x \ge 1\) stones after his move. So after any Bob move, stones remain.
  6. The game ends.Why: every move removes at least 1 stone and there are finitely many stones.
  7. Since the game ends and Bob never takes the last stone (step 5), the last stone is taken by Alice.Why: the players alternate and exactly one of them takes the last stone.

Conclusion. Alice equalises the piles, then copies Bob on the other pile, so she takes the last stone and wins. ∎

2 · Invariants

Invariant: a quantity that no allowed operation changes. If it differs between the start and the target, the target cannot be reached. The trigger is "repeated operations: can state X be reached?"

Make all equal

Six numbers around a circle: \(1,0,1,0,0,0\). A move adds 1 to two neighbouring numbers. Can all six become equal?

Key idea

Alternating sum \(Q = a - b + c - d + e - f\). Two neighbours always have opposite signs in \(Q\).

Solution

Setup. Six numbers sit around a circle; call them \(a, b, c, d, e, f\) in order, so \(f\) is next to \(a\). At the start \(a = 1, b = 0, c = 1, d = 0, e = 0, f = 0\). A move adds 1 to two neighbouring numbers. Define \(Q = a - b + c - d + e - f\).

Claim. No, they can never all be equal.

  1. Any two neighbours appear in \(Q\) with opposite signs.Why: the signs in \(Q\) go \(+, -, +, -, +, -\) around the circle. The neighbouring pairs are \((a,b), (b,c), (c,d), (d,e), (e,f)\) and \((f,a)\). The first five are consecutive in the list, so their signs differ. For \((f, a)\): \(f\) has sign \(-\) and \(a\) has sign \(+\). This works because 6 is even, so the alternation closes up around the circle.
  2. A move does not change \(Q\).Why: a move adds 1 to two neighbours. By step 1 one of them has sign \(+\) and the other \(-\) in \(Q\), so \(Q\) changes by \(+1 - 1 = 0\).
  3. At the start, \(Q = 1 - 0 + 1 - 0 + 0 - 0 = 2\).Why: substitute the starting values.
  4. If all six numbers were equal to some \(t\), then \(Q = t - t + t - t + t - t = 0\).Why: substitute \(a = b = \dots = f = t\).
  5. Reaching an all-equal position would mean \(Q\) changes from 2 (step 3) to 0 (step 4). But \(Q\) never changes (step 2). So it is impossible.Why: a quantity that never changes cannot go from 2 to 0.

Conclusion. \(Q\) is fixed at 2 by every move, but an all-equal circle has \(Q = 0\), so the numbers can never all be equal. ∎

Reaching zero

\(1,2,\dots,50\) are on a board. A move replaces \(a, b\) with \(|a - b|\). Can a single 0 be left at the end?

Key idea

The parity of the sum never changes.

Solution

Setup. The numbers \(1, 2, \dots, 50\) are on the board. A move erases two numbers \(a, b\) and writes \(|a - b|\). Each move reduces the count of numbers by 1, so after 49 moves one number is left. Let \(S\) be the sum of the numbers currently on the board.

Claim. No, the last number can never be 0.

  1. Name the two erased numbers so that \(a \ge b\). Then \(|a - b| = a - b\), and the new sum is \(S' = S - a - b + (a - b) = S - 2b\).Why: we remove \(a\) and \(b\) from the sum and add the new number \(a - b\); the \(a\)'s cancel.
  2. \(S'\) and \(S\) have the same parity (both even or both odd).Why: they differ by \(2b\), which is even (step 1).
  3. At the start, \(S = 1 + 2 + \dots + 50 = \tfrac{50 \cdot 51}{2} = 1275\), which is odd.Why: the formula \(\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\) with \(n = 50\).
  4. So after every move the sum is odd; in particular, the final single number is odd.Why: parity never changes (step 2) and it starts odd (step 3). When one number is left, the sum is that number.
  5. 0 is even, so the last number cannot be 0.Why: an odd number is never 0.

Conclusion. The parity of the sum is fixed and starts odd, so the last number is odd and cannot be 0. ∎

3 · Monovariants

Monovariant: a quantity that moves in only one direction (e.g. strictly increases). You use it to show that a state can never repeat, or that a process must end.

Return configuration

Finitely many stones sit in boxes along an infinite line. A move takes 2 stones from one box and puts one in each neighbouring box. Can you return to the start after \(\ge 1\) moves?

Key idea

Number the boxes with integers. \(Q = \sum_{\text{stones}} (\text{box number})^2\). Every move adds exactly 2 to \(Q\).

Solution

Setup. Boxes sit on a line and are labelled by the integers \(\dots, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\). Finitely many stones are placed in them. A move picks a box \(n\) with at least 2 stones, takes 2 stones out, and puts one in box \(n-1\) and one in box \(n+1\). Define \(Q = \sum_{\text{stones}} (\text{label of the stone's box})^2\). \(Q\) depends only on the current configuration, and it is finite because there are finitely many stones.

Claim. No, the starting configuration can never come back.

  1. One move at box \(n\) changes \(Q\) by \(\Delta Q = (n-1)^2 + (n+1)^2 - 2n^2\).Why: the two removed stones contributed \(n^2\) each, i.e. \(2n^2\). The two new stones contribute \((n-1)^2\) and \((n+1)^2\). Every other stone stays where it is.
  2. \(\Delta Q = 2\).Why: expand: \((n-1)^2 = n^2 - 2n + 1\) and \((n+1)^2 = n^2 + 2n + 1\). Their sum is \(2n^2 + 2\). Subtracting \(2n^2\) leaves 2.
  3. After \(k\) moves, \(Q\) is exactly \(2k\) larger than at the start.Why: each move adds exactly 2 (step 2).
  4. If the start configuration came back after \(k \ge 1\) moves, \(Q\) would equal its starting value. But by step 3 it is larger by \(2k \ge 2\). Contradiction.Why: \(Q\) is determined by the configuration, so the same configuration gives the same \(Q\).

Conclusion. Every move raises \(Q\) by exactly 2, so no configuration can ever repeat. ∎

Infection spread

\(n\times n\) board. A cell becomes infected when \(\ge 2\) of its side-neighbours are infected. Show that fewer than \(n\) infected cells at the start can never infect the whole board.

Key idea

Perimeter = edges between an infected cell and a healthy cell or the board's rim. It never goes up.

Solution

Setup. An \(n\times n\) board. A healthy cell becomes infected when at least 2 of its side-neighbours are infected. The perimeter \(P\) is the number of unit edges that have an infected cell on one side and, on the other side, a healthy cell or the outside of the board. At the start, \(m < n\) cells are infected.

Claim. The whole board can never become infected.

  1. We may infect cells one at a time.Why: if several cells become infected in the same round, each had at least 2 infected neighbours when the round began. Infecting them one by one in any order, each still has at least those 2 infected neighbours when its turn comes, because infections only add infected cells. So it is enough to study a single new infection.
  2. Take one healthy cell \(X\) that becomes infected, and let \(k\) be its number of infected side-neighbours. By the rule, \(k \ge 2\).Why: that is the infection rule.
  3. The \(k\) edges between \(X\) and its infected neighbours leave the perimeter.Why: before, each such edge had an infected cell on one side and the healthy \(X\) on the other, so it counted. After, both sides are infected, so it no longer counts. That is \(-k\).
  4. The other \(4 - k\) edges of \(X\) join the perimeter.Why: each of them has a healthy cell or the board's edge on the far side. Before, neither side was infected, so it did not count. After, \(X\) is infected, so it counts. That is \(+(4 - k)\).
  5. No other edge changes, so \(\Delta P = (4 - k) - k = 4 - 2k \le 0\). (See the figure below.)Why: only \(X\) changed state, so only the 4 edges of \(X\) can change status (steps 3 and 4). Since \(k \ge 2\), \(4 - 2k \le 0\).
  6. So \(P\) never increases during the whole process.Why: step 1 splits the process into single infections, and each one has \(\Delta P \le 0\) (step 5).
  7. At the start, \(P \le 4m < 4n\).Why: each infected cell has 4 edges, so \(m\) cells contribute at most \(4m\) perimeter edges. And \(m < n\).
  8. If the whole board were infected, the perimeter would be exactly the outer boundary of the board: \(P = 4n\).Why: inner edges then have infected cells on both sides and do not count. The boundary has 4 sides of \(n\) edges each.
  9. Going from \(P < 4n\) (step 7) to \(P = 4n\) (step 8) needs \(P\) to increase, which step 6 forbids.Why: contradiction.

Conclusion. The perimeter starts below \(4n\) and never rises, but a fully infected board has perimeter \(4n\). (With exactly \(n\) cells it can work: the diagonal starts at perimeter \(4n\).) ∎

⚠ Course gap: it states that the perimeter can't increase without the \(4 - 2k\) count. Steps 3–5 give it.

4 · Pigeonhole principle

  1. If \(n+1\) objects go into \(n\) boxes, some box holds \(\ge 2\) objects.
  2. If more than \(kn\) objects go into \(n\) boxes, some box holds \(\ge k+1\) objects.
  3. If infinitely many objects go into finitely many boxes, some box holds infinitely many.

What to find: the boxes.

Same number of friends

\(n \ge 2\) people. Friendship is mutual. Show that two people have the same number of friends.

Key idea

Friend counts lie in \(\{0,\dots,n-1\}\), but \(0\) and \(n-1\) cannot both occur.

Solution

Setup. There are \(n \ge 2\) people. Friendship is mutual, and nobody is their own friend. For a person \(x\), let \(f(x)\) be the number of friends of \(x\).

Claim. Two people have the same number of friends.

  1. Each \(f(x)\) is one of \(0, 1, \dots, n-1\). That is \(n\) possible values.Why: \(x\) can be friends only with the other \(n - 1\) people.
  2. The values 0 and \(n - 1\) cannot both occur.Why: suppose someone \(Y\) has \(f(Y) = n - 1\). Then \(Y\) is friends with every other person. Since friendship is mutual, every other person has \(Y\) as a friend, so has at least 1 friend. And \(Y\) has \(n - 1 \ge 1\) friends. So nobody has 0 friends.
  3. So the \(n\) values \(f(x)\) all lie in a set of at most \(n - 1\) numbers: either \(\{0, \dots, n-2\}\) or \(\{1, \dots, n-1\}\).Why: step 1 with one of the two end values removed (step 2).
  4. Two people have the same value.Why: pigeonhole: \(n\) people placed into at most \(n - 1\) values means some value is used twice.

Conclusion. Friend counts have only \(n-1\) usable values for \(n\) people, so two counts coincide. ∎

Ant cover

51 ants on a \(1\times1\) table. Show that a \(\tfrac15\times\tfrac15\) card can be placed to cover \(\ge 3\) ants.

Key idea

Split the table into 25 small squares. \(51 > 2\cdot25\).

Solution

Setup. The table is the unit square \([0,1]\times[0,1]\). The 51 ants are 51 points on it. The card is a \(\tfrac15\times\tfrac15\) square.

Claim. The card can be placed to cover at least 3 ants.

  1. Cut the table into a \(5\times5\) grid of closed squares of side \(\tfrac15\). There are 25 of them, and every point of the table lies in at least one. Assign each ant to one square that contains it (if it sits on a shared edge, pick either square).Why: \(5 \cdot \tfrac15 = 1\), so 5 strips in each direction cover the table exactly.
  2. Some square has at least 3 ants.Why: if every square had at most 2 ants, there would be at most \(25 \cdot 2 = 50\) ants. But there are 51.
  3. Place the card exactly on that square. It covers the whole closed square, so it covers those \(\ge 3\) ants.Why: the card and the square have the same size \(\tfrac15\times\tfrac15\).

Conclusion. By pigeonhole one of the 25 small squares holds 3 ants, and the card fits exactly on it. ∎

Arrows on a grid

\(100\times100\) board with walls all round, except the right side of the top-right cell. Each cell has an arrow (↑↓←→). Each second the insect steps along its cell's arrow (if a wall blocks it, it stays), and that arrow then turns 90° clockwise. Can the insect stay on the board forever?

Key idea

Infinite pigeonhole: some cell is visited infinitely often. Its arrow cycles through all four directions, so this spreads to every neighbour, and so to every cell.

Solution

Setup. The board has \(100\times100\) cells, with walls all round except on the right side of the top-right cell \(R\) (the exit). Each cell holds an arrow (↑ → ↓ ←). Each second the insect tries to step one cell in the direction of the arrow in its current cell. If a wall is in the way, it stays put. Then the arrow of that cell turns 90° clockwise.

Claim. No. The insect always leaves the board.

  1. Suppose, for contradiction, that the insect stays on the board forever.Why: we will derive a contradiction.
  2. Some cell \(C\) is visited infinitely often.Why: the insect is in some cell at each of infinitely many seconds, but there are only \(10^4\) cells. If each cell were visited finitely often, the total number of visits would be finite (infinite pigeonhole).
  3. Each time the insect acts from \(C\), it uses \(C\)'s current arrow and then the arrow turns 90°. So the directions it uses from \(C\) repeat the cycle ↑, →, ↓, ←, ↑, … (starting wherever the arrow started). Since it acts from \(C\) infinitely often, it uses each of the 4 directions from \(C\) infinitely often.Why: four clockwise quarter turns bring the arrow back to where it started, so the directions cycle with period 4.
  4. Every side-neighbour \(D\) of \(C\) on the board is visited infinitely often.Why: between two cells of the board there is no wall. Whenever the insect at \(C\) uses the direction pointing to \(D\) (which happens infinitely often by step 3), it steps into \(D\).
  5. Every cell of the board is visited infinitely often; in particular, the top-right cell \(R\).Why: any cell can be reached from \(C\) by a path of side-steps. Apply step 4 along the path, one cell at a time.
  6. At some visit to \(R\), its arrow points right, and the insect steps through the opening and leaves the board.Why: apply step 3 to \(R\): the right direction is used infinitely often. The right side of \(R\) has no wall.
  7. Step 6 contradicts step 1.Why: the insect was assumed never to leave.

Conclusion. Infinite pigeonhole gives a cell visited infinitely often; its rotating arrow spreads that to every cell, including the exit cell, whose arrow eventually points out. ∎

5 · Proof by colouring

Colour the cells so that every piece or move covers or changes colours in a fixed way. Then compare colour counts. The chessboard colouring is the usual choice; for \(1\times k\) pieces, colour \((i+j) \bmod k\).

Mutilated chessboard

Remove two opposite corners from an \(8\times8\) board. Can 31 dominoes cover the remaining 62 cells?

Key idea

Every domino covers 1 black cell and 1 white cell. Opposite corners have the same colour.

Solution

Setup. Label the cells \((i, j)\) with row \(i\) and column \(j\) from 1 to 8. Colour \((i,j)\) dark if \(i + j\) is even and light if it is odd. Remove \((1,1)\) and \((8,8)\), leaving 62 cells. A domino covers two side-adjacent cells.

Claim. No, 31 dominoes cannot cover the 62 cells.

  1. The full board has 32 dark and 32 light cells.Why: in each row the colours alternate along 8 cells, giving 4 of each. With 8 rows that is 32 of each.
  2. Both removed cells are dark.Why: \(1 + 1 = 2\) and \(8 + 8 = 16\) are even.
  3. The remaining 62 cells are 30 dark and 32 light.Why: subtract the two dark cells (step 2) from step 1.
  4. Every domino covers exactly one dark and one light cell.Why: side-adjacent cells differ by 1 in exactly one coordinate, so \(i + j\) changes by 1 and its parity flips.
  5. 31 non-overlapping dominoes would cover exactly 31 dark cells.Why: each covers one dark cell (step 4).
  6. There are only 30 dark cells (step 3), and \(31 \ne 30\). Contradiction.Why: the dominoes must cover exactly the remaining cells.

Conclusion. Removing two same-coloured corners leaves 30 dark and 32 light cells, but dominoes always cover colours in equal numbers. ∎

Corner-to-corner walk

Walk on an \(8\times8\) board, stepping between side-adjacent cells, visiting each cell exactly once. Can you start and end at opposite corners?

Key idea

Every step changes colour, and there are 63 steps (odd).

Solution

Setup. Colour the \(8\times8\) board as in the previous problem: \((i,j)\) is dark if \(i + j\) is even. A walk visits cells \(c_1, c_2, \dots, c_{64}\), each exactly once, moving each time to a side-adjacent cell.

Claim. No, the walk cannot start and end at opposite corners.

  1. Each step moves to a cell of the other colour.Why: a side step changes \(i + j\) by 1, which flips its parity.
  2. So \(c_1, c_3, c_5, \dots\) (odd positions) all have the colour of \(c_1\), and \(c_2, c_4, \dots\) (even positions) all have the other colour.Why: the colours alternate along the walk (step 1).
  3. \(c_{64}\) has the other colour from \(c_1\).Why: 64 is even (step 2).
  4. Opposite corners have the same colour: \((1,1)\) and \((8,8)\) are both dark (sums 2 and 16), and \((1,8)\) and \((8,1)\) are both light (sums 9 and 9).Why: compute \(i + j\) for each corner.
  5. So \(c_1\) and \(c_{64}\) cannot be opposite corners.Why: that would need the same colour (step 4) but step 3 needs different colours.

Conclusion. 63 steps flip the colour an odd number of times, so the walk ends on the opposite colour, while opposite corners share a colour. ∎

Unequal neighbours

An \(m\times n\) grid of integers. You may add 1 to any cell, at most once per cell. Can you always make every pair of side-neighbours different?

Key idea

Chessboard colouring: make black cells even and white cells odd.

Solution

Setup. An \(m\times n\) grid holds an integer in each cell. We may add 1 to any cell, at most once per cell. Goal: every two side-neighbours hold different numbers. Colour cell \((i,j)\) dark if \(i + j\) is even and light if odd.

Claim. Yes, it is always possible.

  1. Side-neighbours always have different colours.Why: a side step changes \(i + j\) by 1.
  2. For each dark cell: if its number is odd, add 1; if it is even, leave it. For each light cell: if its number is even, add 1; if it is odd, leave it.Why: each cell is changed at most once, which is allowed.
  3. Now every dark cell holds an even number and every light cell holds an odd number.Why: adding 1 turns odd into even and even into odd; the cells we left alone already had the right parity.
  4. Any two side-neighbours hold different numbers.Why: one is dark and one is light (step 1), so one holds an even and the other an odd number (step 3), and an even number never equals an odd number.

Conclusion. Force dark cells to be even and light cells to be odd; neighbours then have different parities, so they differ. ∎

\(6\times6\) with \(1\times4\) tiles

Can \(1\times4\) tiles cover a \(6\times6\) board?

Key idea

Give cell \((i,j)\) the colour \((i+j) \bmod 4\). Every \(1\times4\) tile covers one cell of each colour.

Solution

Setup. Index rows \(i\) and columns \(j\) from 0 to 5. Give cell \((i, j)\) the colour \((i + j) \bmod 4\), one of 0, 1, 2, 3. A \(1\times4\) tile covers 4 consecutive cells of one row or one column.

Claim. No, the \(6\times6\) board cannot be tiled.

  1. Every tile covers exactly one cell of each colour.Why: a horizontal tile covers \((i, j), (i, j+1), (i, j+2), (i, j+3)\), whose values of \(i + j\) are four consecutive integers \(s, s+1, s+2, s+3\). Four consecutive integers have four different remainders mod 4, i.e. one each of 0, 1, 2, 3. A vertical tile is the same with \(i\) increasing.
  2. A tiling uses \(36 / 4 = 9\) tiles, so it covers exactly 9 cells of each colour. So a tiling is only possible if the board has exactly 9 cells of each colour.Why: each tile covers one of each colour (step 1), and the tiles cover every cell exactly once.
  3. The number of cells with \(i + j = s\) is \(s + 1\) for \(s \le 5\) and \(11 - s\) for \(s \ge 5\).Why: for \(s \le 5\), \(i\) can be \(0, 1, \dots, s\) (then \(j = s - i\) is between 0 and 5). For \(s \ge 5\), \(i\) can be \(s - 5, \dots, 5\).
  4. Colour 1 has \(i + j \in \{1, 5, 9\}\): \(2 + 6 + 2 = 10\) cells. Colour 3 has \(i + j \in \{3, 7\}\): \(4 + 4 = 8\) cells.Why: \(i + j\) ranges from 0 to 10; use step 3 for each sum.
  5. \(10 \ne 9\) (and \(8 \ne 9\)), which contradicts step 2.Why: a tiling needs exactly 9 of each colour.

Conclusion. Each \(1\times4\) tile uses every colour once, but the board has 10 cells of colour 1 and 8 of colour 3. ∎

6 · Induction

  1. Weak: prove \(P(n_0)\). Prove \(P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+1)\).
  2. Strong: prove \(P(n_0)\). Prove that \(P(n_0),\dots,P(n)\) together give \(P(n+1)\).
  3. Step 2: for odd \(n\) only, prove \(P(1)\) and \(P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+2)\).

When to use it: small cases show a pattern, or the \((n+1)\) case reduces to the \(n\) case.

Sum \(1 + \dots + n\)

Prove that \(\displaystyle 1 + 2 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\).

Key idea

Weak induction: add \(n+1\) to both sides. Or see it: two staircases make a rectangle.

Solution

Setup. Let \(S(n) = 1 + 2 + \dots + n\).

Claim. \(S(n) = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\) for every \(n \ge 1\).

  1. Base case \(n = 1\): \(S(1) = 1\), and \(\tfrac{1 \cdot 2}{2} = 1\). They agree.Why: substitute \(n = 1\).
  2. Inductive step: assume \(S(n) = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\) for some \(n \ge 1\). Then \(S(n+1) = S(n) + (n + 1)\).Why: \(S(n+1)\) is \(S(n)\) with the next number \(n+1\) added.
  3. \[S(n+1) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + (n+1) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + \frac{2(n+1)}{2} = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}.\]Why: substitute the assumption, write \(n + 1\) over the common denominator 2, then factor out \((n + 1)\).
  4. This is the formula with \(n + 1\) in place of \(n\). By induction it holds for all \(n \ge 1\).Why: base case (step 1) plus inductive step (steps 2–3).

Conclusion. \(1 + \dots + n = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\). (The picture in step 4 is a second proof: two staircases fill an \(n\times(n+1)\) rectangle.) ∎

Gas cans on a circular track

\(N\) cans on a one-way loop hold, in total, exactly one lap's worth of fuel. You start with an empty tank. Show that some start point lets you finish the lap.

Key idea

Some can \(i\) holds enough fuel to reach the next can. Merge can \(i+1\) into can \(i\) and induct on \(N\).

Solution

Setup. The loop has length 1 (one lap) and is driven clockwise. There are \(N\) cans, numbered \(1, \dots, N\) clockwise. Can \(i\) holds enough fuel to drive a distance \(x_i\), and the road from can \(i\) to the next can is \(y_i\) long (after can \(N\) comes can 1). The total fuel is one lap and the gaps make one lap: \(\sum x_i = \sum y_i = 1\). You start at some can with an empty tank, pick up its fuel, and collect each can's fuel as you pass it.

Claim. For every \(N \ge 1\), some starting can lets you finish the lap. We prove it by induction on \(N\).

  1. Base \(N = 1\): the single can has \(x_1 = 1\) and \(y_1 = 1\). Start there, pick up fuel for distance 1, and drive the lap of length 1.Why: \(\sum x_i = \sum y_i = 1\) with one term each.
  2. For any \(N\), some can \(i\) has \(x_i \ge y_i\).Why: if every \(x_i < y_i\), adding up would give \(\sum x_i < \sum y_i\), i.e. \(1 < 1\), which is false.
  3. Inductive step (\(N \ge 2\)): assume the claim for \(N - 1\) cans. Take \(i\) from step 2 and build a smaller track: remove can \(i+1\) and pour its fuel into can \(i\). Can \(i\) now holds \(x_i + x_{i+1}\), and the gap from can \(i\) to can \(i+2\) is \(y_i + y_{i+1}\). This track has \(N - 1\) cans, total fuel 1 and total length 1, so by the hypothesis some starting can \(s\) works on it.Why: the merge moves fuel and joins two gaps, so the totals are unchanged.
  4. Start at the same can \(s\) on the original track. Up to arriving at can \(i\), the two drives are identical.Why: the cans before \(i\) are the same on both tracks. (\(s \ne i+1\), because can \(i+1\) does not exist on the small track.)
  5. Arriving at can \(i\) with tank \(T \ge 0\), the original track lets you reach can \(i + 1\): after picking up \(x_i\) the tank is \(T + x_i \ge y_i\).Why: \(T \ge 0\) and \(x_i \ge y_i\) (step 2). While driving the gap, the tank is at least \(T + x_i - y_i \ge 0\).
  6. At can \(i + 1\) the original tank is \(T + x_i - y_i + x_{i+1}\). This equals the small track's tank at the same point of the road, \(T + (x_i + x_{i+1}) - y_i\). From there on the two tracks are identical again.Why: both have collected the same fuel and driven the same distance \(y_i\) from can \(i\).
  7. So the original tank is never empty wherever the small track's tank is not, and the start \(s\) completes the lap on the original track too.Why: steps 4–6 cover the whole lap. This completes the induction.

Conclusion. Some can has enough fuel to reach the next one; merging them gives a smaller track with a working start, which also works on the original. ∎

Two-colouring regions cut by lines

\(n\) lines cut the plane into regions. Show that two colours suffice so that regions sharing an edge differ.

Key idea

Add one line and swap every colour on one side of it.

Solution

Setup. \(n\) lines cut the plane into regions. A colouring gives each region black or white. It is good if any two regions that share a boundary segment (not just a single point) get different colours.

Claim. For every \(n \ge 1\) a good colouring exists. We prove it by induction on \(n\).

  1. Base \(n = 1\): one line gives two half-planes. Colour one black and the other white. They share the line and differ.Why: there are only two regions.
  2. Inductive step: assume every arrangement of \(n\) lines has a good colouring. Take \(n + 1\) lines, remove one of them, \(L\), and colour the regions of the other \(n\) lines well.Why: that is the hypothesis.
  3. Put \(L\) back. It cuts some regions into two pieces. Swap the colour of every region on one side of \(L\) (say the right) and leave the left side alone.Why: this defines the new colouring.
  4. Two regions sharing a segment that is not on \(L\) still differ.Why: the shared segment lies on one side of \(L\), so both regions lie on that side. Each is (part of) an old region, and the two old regions shared that segment, so they had different colours. Both were swapped or neither was, so they still differ.
  5. Two regions sharing a segment that is on \(L\) now differ.Why: they are the two halves of one old region that \(L\) cut in two, so they had the same colour. Exactly one of them (the right one) was swapped.
  6. Every shared segment is either on \(L\) or not, so by steps 4 and 5 the new colouring is good. This completes the induction.Why: the two cases cover everything.

Conclusion. Adding a line and flipping all colours on one side of it keeps the colouring good, so two colours always suffice. ∎

Splitting 1000 coins

Split a pile into \(x + y\) and score \(xy\). Keep splitting until every pile is 1. Show that the total does not depend on the choices, and find it.

Key idea

Small cases give \(0, 1, 3, 6 \to \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\). Prove it by strong induction.

Solution

Setup. A pile of \(x + y\) coins is split into piles of \(x\) and \(y\) (both \(\ge 1\)), scoring \(xy\). Splitting continues until every pile has 1 coin. Let \(f(n)\) be the total score starting from one pile of \(n\) coins.

Claim. \(f(n) = \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\), whatever splits are chosen. So \(f(1000) = 499500\).

  1. Base \(n = 1\): no split is possible, so \(f(1) = 0 = \tfrac{1\cdot0}{2}\).Why: a pile of 1 is already finished.
  2. Strong induction: let \(n \ge 2\) and assume the claim for every pile smaller than \(n\). The first split is \(n = x + y\) with \(x, y \ge 1\), scoring \(xy\). After that, the two piles are split independently and score \(f(x)\) and \(f(y)\).Why: the later splits of one pile never involve the other pile.
  3. So the total is \(xy + \tfrac{x(x-1)}{2} + \tfrac{y(y-1)}{2}\).Why: \(x, y < n\), so the hypothesis applies to both.
  4. \[xy + \frac{x(x-1)}{2} + \frac{y(y-1)}{2} = \frac{2xy + x^2 - x + y^2 - y}{2} = \frac{(x+y)^2 - (x+y)}{2} = \frac{n^2 - n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}.\]Why: put everything over 2, use \(x^2 + 2xy + y^2 = (x+y)^2\), and substitute \(x + y = n\).
  5. The result does not depend on \(x\), so it does not depend on any choice. With \(n = 1000\): \(\tfrac{1000 \cdot 999}{2} = 499500\).Why: step 4 holds for every first split, and the hypothesis covers every later choice.

Conclusion. The total score is always \(\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\); for 1000 coins it is 499500. ∎

Second proof. Think of each pair of coins. When a pile of \(x + y\) splits into \(x\) and \(y\), exactly the \(xy\) pairs with one coin in each part get separated. Every pair starts together and ends apart, and once apart it stays apart, so it is separated exactly once. So the total score is the number of pairs, \(\binom{n}{2} = \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\).

⚠ Course says “simplifying” and skips the algebra. Step 4 shows it.

Water pistols (\(n\) odd)

\(n\) people, all pairwise distances distinct. Each person shoots the nearest person. If \(n\) is odd, show that someone stays dry.

Key idea

The closest pair \(A, B\) shoot each other. Remove them and use induction on odd \(n\).

Solution

Setup. \(n\) people stand so that all pairwise distances are different. Each person shoots the nearest other person (unique, since distances differ). \(n\) is odd.

Claim. Someone is not hit. We prove it by induction over odd \(n\): \(1, 3, 5, \dots\)

  1. Base \(n = 1\): there is nobody to shoot, so the one person is not hit.Why: no shots at all.
  2. Inductive step: let \(n = k + 2\) with \(k\) odd, and assume the claim for \(k\) people. Let \(A, B\) be the closest pair (the smallest of all distances). Then \(A\) shoots \(B\) and \(B\) shoots \(A\).Why: every other person is farther from \(A\) than \(B\) is, because \(AB\) is the smallest distance; the same for \(B\).
  3. Let \(G\) be the other \(k\) people. Shots that hit people in \(G\) come only from people in \(G\).Why: \(A\) and \(B\) shoot each other (step 2).
  4. Case 1: someone in \(G\) shoots \(A\) or \(B\). Then at most \(k - 1\) shots land on the \(k\) people of \(G\), so someone in \(G\) is not hit.Why: \(G\) fires \(k\) shots in total (step 3), and at least one of them goes outside \(G\).
  5. Case 2: nobody in \(G\) shoots \(A\) or \(B\). Then each person in \(G\) shoots their nearest person, who is in \(G\), so \(G\) plays exactly the \(k\)-person game. By the hypothesis someone in \(G\) is not hit by \(G\), and \(A, B\) do not shoot \(G\), so that person is not hit at all.Why: a person's nearest person overall, if it lies in \(G\), is also their nearest within \(G\). And \(k\) is odd, so the hypothesis applies.
  6. The two cases cover everything, so someone is dry. This completes the induction.Why: either some shot from \(G\) leaves \(G\), or none does.

Conclusion. The closest pair shoot each other; the rest either waste a shot on them or form a smaller odd game, and either way someone stays dry. ∎

7 · Symmetry

Pair each object with its mirror image. Either it hands you information for free (pairs sum to a constant, halves of the work are equal), or it is the whole argument (copy-strategies in games).

Placing coins

Players take turns placing identical coins on a rectangular table, with no overlaps. The last player able to place a coin wins. Alice goes first. Who wins?

Key idea

Alice takes the centre, then mirrors every move of Bob's through the centre.

Solution

Setup. A rectangular table with centre \(O\). Coins are identical discs of radius \(r\), and a coin must lie entirely on the table without overlapping another coin (touching is allowed). The player who cannot place a coin loses. Let \(\sigma\) be the half-turn about \(O\): it sends each point \(P\) to the point \(\sigma(P)\) on the other side of \(O\) at the same distance. \(\sigma\) maps the rectangle onto itself. (We assume a coin fits on the table at all; if the table is at least \(2r\) wide in both directions, a coin fits centred at \(O\).)

Claim. Alice wins.

  1. Alice's first coin is centred at \(O\).Why: legal on an empty table (see the setup).
  2. Alice's rule afterwards: when Bob places a coin centred at \(P\), Alice places one centred at \(\sigma(P)\).Why: this is the strategy we check.
  3. After each of Alice's moves, the set of coins is symmetric under \(\sigma\): if a coin is centred at \(Q\), a coin is centred at \(\sigma(Q)\).Why: true after step 1 because \(\sigma(O) = O\). Step 2 adds coins in pairs \(P, \sigma(P)\), so it stays true, as long as Alice's move is legal (steps 4–6).
  4. Alice's coin at \(\sigma(P)\) lies on the table.Why: Bob's coin at \(P\) lies on the table, and \(\sigma\) maps the table onto itself and his disc onto the disc at \(\sigma(P)\).
  5. Alice's coin does not overlap any coin placed before Bob's move.Why: suppose it overlapped a coin at \(Q\). Applying \(\sigma\), Bob's disc at \(P\) would overlap the disc at \(\sigma(Q)\), which is an existing coin by step 3. That contradicts Bob's move being legal.
  6. Alice's coin does not overlap Bob's new coin.Why: if the discs at \(P\) and \(\sigma(P)\) overlapped, their centres would be less than \(2r\) apart. That distance is \(2|PO|\), so \(|PO| < r\), and then Bob's disc would overlap Alice's first coin at \(O\) (centres less than \(2r\) apart). That contradicts Bob's move being legal.
  7. So after every Bob move, Alice has a legal move.Why: steps 4–6.
  8. The game ends.Why: each coin covers area \(\pi r^2\) of a table with finite area, and coins don't overlap.
  9. The player who is stuck is Bob.Why: the game ends (step 8), and by step 7 it can never end with Alice unable to move.

Conclusion. Alice takes the centre and answers every coin with its mirror image through the centre, so Bob runs out of moves first. ∎

Sum of five-digit palindromes

Find the sum of all numbers \(\overline{abcba}\) with \(a \ne 0\).

Key idea

Pair \(\overline{abcba}\) with \(\overline{a'b'c'b'a'}\), where \(a' = 10-a,\ b' = 9-b,\ c' = 9-c\). Each pair sums to 110000.

Solution

Setup. A five-digit palindrome is written \(\overline{abcba}\): \(a\) is the first and last digit, \(b\) the second and fourth, \(c\) the middle. \(a \in \{1, \dots, 9\}\) (a leading 0 would make it a four-digit number) and \(b, c \in \{0, \dots, 9\}\).

Claim. The sum is \(49\,500\,000\).

  1. \(\overline{abcba} = 10001a + 1010b + 100c\).Why: place values: \(a\) sits in the \(10^4\) and \(10^0\) places, giving \(10000a + a = 10001a\); \(b\) sits in the \(10^3\) and \(10^1\) places, giving \(1000b + 10b = 1010b\); \(c\) sits in the \(10^2\) place, giving \(100c\).
  2. There are \(9 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 900\) five-digit palindromes.Why: 9 choices for \(a\), 10 for \(b\), 10 for \(c\), chosen independently.
  3. Pair each palindrome with a partner: \(a' = 10 - a\), \(b' = 9 - b\), \(c' = 9 - c\). The partner \(\overline{a'b'c'b'a'}\) is again a five-digit palindrome.Why: \(a \in \{1..9\}\) gives \(10 - a \in \{1..9\}\), so the partner's first digit is not 0. \(b \in \{0..9\}\) gives \(9 - b \in \{0..9\}\), and the same for \(c\).
  4. The partner of the partner is the original.Why: \(10 - (10 - a) = a\), \(9 - (9 - b) = b\), \(9 - (9 - c) = c\).
  5. No palindrome is its own partner.Why: that would need \(b = 9 - b\), i.e. \(2b = 9\), i.e. \(b = 4.5\), which is not a digit.
  6. So the 900 palindromes split into 450 disjoint pairs \(\{N, N'\}\).Why: by steps 4 and 5, partnering matches each palindrome with exactly one other, different palindrome.
  7. Each pair sums to 110000:\[N + N' = 10001(a + a') + 1010(b + b') + 100(c + c') = 10001\cdot10 + 1010\cdot9 + 100\cdot9 = 100010 + 9090 + 900 = 110000.\]Why: add the two expressions from step 1; \(a + a' = 10\), \(b + b' = 9\), \(c + c' = 9\) by the definition in step 3.
  8. Total \(= 450 \cdot 110000 = 49\,500\,000\).Why: 450 pairs (step 6), each summing to 110000 (step 7).

Conclusion. The palindromes pair up into 450 pairs of sum 110000, so the total is \(49\,500\,000\). ∎

⚠ Course says “n-th + n-th-from-last = 110000” without proof. Steps 3–6 give the explicit pairing that justifies it.

Average median of subsets

\(S = \{1,\dots,100\}\). Take the median of every non-empty subset. What is the average?

Key idea

Reflect \(x \mapsto 101 - x\). That sends a subset with median \(m\) to one with median \(101 - m\).

Solution

Setup. \(S = \{1, \dots, 100\}\). For a non-empty subset \(B\), \(\operatorname{med} B\) is the middle element if \(|B|\) is odd, and the average of the two middle elements if \(|B|\) is even. Let \(\mathcal{B}\) be the set of all non-empty subsets and \(N = |\mathcal{B}| = 2^{100} - 1\). We want \(\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}} \operatorname{med} B\).

Claim. The average is \(50.5\).

  1. Define \(r(B) = \{101 - x : x \in B\}\). It is again a non-empty subset of \(S\).Why: \(x \in \{1..100\}\) gives \(101 - x \in \{1..100\}\), and \(r(B)\) has as many elements as \(B\).
  2. \(r(r(B)) = B\), so \(r\) is a bijection from \(\mathcal{B}\) to itself.Why: \(101 - (101 - x) = x\). A map that undoes itself is one-to-one and onto.
  3. \(\operatorname{med} r(B) = 101 - \operatorname{med} B\).Why: list \(B\) in increasing order, \(b_1 < \dots < b_k\). Then \(r(B)\) in increasing order is \(101 - b_k < \dots < 101 - b_1\): the order reverses. The middle position of a list is still the middle position when the list is reversed, so for odd \(k\) the median becomes \(101 - (\text{old median})\). For even \(k\) the two middle elements \(u, v\) become \(101 - v, 101 - u\), whose average is \(101 - \tfrac{u+v}{2}\).
  4. \(\sum_{B} \operatorname{med} B = \sum_{B} \operatorname{med} r(B)\).Why: as \(B\) runs over \(\mathcal{B}\), \(r(B)\) also runs over all of \(\mathcal{B}\), each set exactly once (step 2). So the right side is the same sum in a different order.
  5. \(\sum_{B} \operatorname{med} r(B) = \sum_{B} \big(101 - \operatorname{med} B\big) = 101N - \sum_{B} \operatorname{med} B\).Why: step 3, then split the sum; there are \(N\) terms of 101.
  6. Let \(M = \sum_B \operatorname{med} B\). Steps 4 and 5 give \(M = 101N - M\), so \(2M = 101N\) and \(M / N = \tfrac{101}{2} = 50.5\).Why: solve the linear equation.

Conclusion. Reflecting every subset through \(x \mapsto 101 - x\) pairs medians that sum to 101, so the average median is \(50.5\). ∎

⚠ Course pairs \(B\) with \(r(B)\) but only treats \(B = S\) as self-paired. Many subsets are their own reflection (e.g. \(\{1,100\}\)). Summing over the bijection, as in steps 4–6, handles all of them at once.
