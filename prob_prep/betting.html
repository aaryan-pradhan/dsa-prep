1 · Plain expected value

Fair price \(= \mathbb{E}[\text{payout}]\). Write the payout as a random variable, then use linearity and indicators.

Known urn (10B, 10W), $10 for the right colour

Key idea

\(P(\text{win}) = \tfrac12\) whichever colour you call.

Solution

Setup. 10 black and 10 white balls; you call a colour and win $10 if the drawn ball has that colour.

  1. Whichever colour you call, \(P(\text{win}) = \tfrac{10}{20} = \tfrac12\).Why: 10 of the 20 equally likely balls match.
  2. \(\mathbb{E}[\text{payout}] = 10\cdot\tfrac12 = \) $5.Why: fair price = expected payout.

Conclusion. The fair price is $5. ∎

Secret urn, unknown mix

Key idea

Call your colour with a fair coin flip. Then \(P(\text{win}) = \tfrac12\) whatever the mix is.

Solution

Setup. A friend fills the urn with an unknown fraction \(p\) of black balls. You call a colour and win $10 if the drawn ball matches.

  1. Call black or white by flipping a fair coin, independently of everything.Why: this is the strategy.
  2. \(P(\text{win}) = \tfrac12p + \tfrac12(1 - p) = \tfrac12\), for every \(p\).Why: call black (probability \(\tfrac12\)) and draw black (probability \(p\)), or call white and draw white.
  3. The fair fee is \(10\cdot\tfrac12 = \) $5, guaranteed whatever the friend chose.Why: step 2 doesn't depend on \(p\).

Conclusion. Randomising the call guarantees a $5 value. ∎

⚠ Course assumes \(p \sim U[0,1]\). Nothing justifies that: an adversarial friend could pick \(p = 0\) against a fixed call of “black”. Randomising the call (step 1) gives \(\tfrac12\) with no assumption.

$10 per red card before the first spade

Key idea

A red card precedes all 13 spades with probability \(\tfrac{1}{14}\).

Solution

Setup. A shuffled deck is turned over; you get $10 for each red card that appears before the first spade. Let \(X\) = the number of such red cards.

  1. \(X = \sum_{\text{26 reds } c} I_c\), where \(I_c = 1\) if red card \(c\) comes before all 13 spades.Why: count the reds before the first spade one at a time.
  2. \(P(I_c = 1) = \tfrac{1}{14}\).Why: the relative order of \(c\) and the 13 spades is uniform, so \(c\) is first among these 14 cards with probability \(\tfrac1{14}\).
  3. \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \tfrac{26}{14} = \tfrac{13}{7}\), and the fair fee is \(10\cdot\tfrac{13}{7} = \tfrac{130}{7} \approx\) $18.57.Why: linearity.

Conclusion. The fair fee is about $18.57. ∎

2 · Present vs future

  1. At each decision: stop if (value now) \(\ge \mathbb{E}[\text{value of continuing optimally}]\); otherwise continue.
  2. With a fixed number of steps, work backwards from the last step.
  3. One-step look-ahead ("stop as soon as stopping beats exactly one more step") is optimal when the problem is monotone: once stopping is better, it stays better.

Dice game: up to 2 rolls, then up to 3

Key idea

Roll: keep it and stop, or throw it away and play the game with one roll fewer. Keep iff it beats that.

Solution

Setup. You may roll a die up to \(k\) times. After each roll you either keep that value as your payout and stop, or throw it away and continue; the last allowed roll must be kept.

  1. Level: rec\((k)\) = the expected payout with \(k\) rolls left, playing optimally.Why: only the number of rolls left matters.
  2. Choice: after seeing roll \(x\), keep \(x\) or reroll (which is worth rec\((k-1)\)).Why: the rules.
  3. Check: \(k = 1\): you must keep it, so rec\((1) = 3.5\).Why: the mean of one roll.
  4. Move: \(\text{rec}(k) = \tfrac16\sum_{x=1}^{6}\max\big(x, \text{rec}(k-1)\big)\).Why: for each \(x\), take the better option.
double rec(ll k) {
    if (k == 1) return 3.5;
    double ans = 0;
    for (ll x = 1; x <= 6; x++) ans += max((double)x, rec(k - 1)) / 6;
    return ans;
}   // rec(2), rec(3)
  1. 2 rolls: keep \(x \ge 4\) (since \(4 > 3.5\)), otherwise reroll. \(\text{rec}(2) = \tfrac16(3.5 + 3.5 + 3.5 + 4 + 5 + 6) = \tfrac{25.5}{6} = \) \(4.25\).Why: the three low rolls are replaced by the value 3.5 of rerolling.
  2. 3 rolls: keep \(x \ge 5\) (since \(5 > 4.25 > 4\)), otherwise play the 2-roll game. \(\text{rec}(3) = \tfrac16(4\cdot4.25 + 5 + 6) = \tfrac{28}{6} = \) \(\tfrac{14}{3} \approx 4.67\).Why: the four low rolls are replaced by 4.25.

Conclusion. With 2 rolls the game is worth 4.25; with 3 rolls, \(\tfrac{14}{3}\). ∎

Push your luck with a d10

1–9 adds that many $ to your total; 10 loses everything and ends the game. You may quit after any roll. At what total should you quit?

Key idea

One more roll changes the expected total from \(x\) to \(0.9(x + 5)\).

Solution

Setup. Each roll of a d10: 1–9 adds that many dollars to your total \(x\); 10 loses everything and ends the game. You may quit after any roll and keep \(x\).

  1. If you roll once more from total \(x\): with probability \(\tfrac{9}{10}\) you gain a uniform amount from 1 to 9 (mean 5), and with probability \(\tfrac{1}{10}\) you drop to 0. So the expected total after one more roll is \(\tfrac{9}{10}(x + 5)\).Why: law of total expectation.
  2. One more roll beats quitting exactly when \(\tfrac{9}{10}(x + 5) > x\), i.e. \(0.9x + 4.5 > x\), i.e. \(x < 45\).Why: rearrange.
  3. The problem is monotone: while you play, your total only goes up, so once \(x \ge 45\) it stays \(\ge 45\), and quitting stays better than one more roll. For monotone problems the one-step look-ahead rule is optimal.Why: see the idea box of §2.
  4. So quit at the first total \(\ge\) $45 (at exactly 45 you are indifferent).Why: steps 2–3.

Conclusion. Keep rolling while your total is below $45. ∎

⚠ Course compares with just one more roll but never says why that is enough. Step 3 is the reason.

3 · Optimal parameters

Describe a family of strategies with a parameter \(\theta\) (a threshold, a fraction), compute \(\mathbb{E}(\theta)\) or \(P(\theta)\), then maximise. First argue that the optimum lies in that family.

Secretary problem, \(n = 6\)

Maximise \(P(\text{hiring the best})\).

Key idea

Skip the first \(k\) candidates, then hire the first one who is best so far. The best candidate is hired iff the best of the first \(i-1\) is among the skipped \(k\).

Solution

Setup. 6 candidates arrive in random order; after each interview you must hire or reject for good. Rule with threshold \(k\): reject the first \(k\), then hire the first candidate who is better than everyone seen so far.

  1. A threshold rule is optimal.Why: only a best-so-far candidate can be the overall best. If the \(i\)-th candidate is best so far, she is the overall best with probability \(\tfrac{1/6}{1/i} = \tfrac i6\), which grows with \(i\). And whether a later best-so-far appears doesn't depend on the ranks seen so far. So once it is right to hire a best-so-far candidate, it stays right.
  2. With threshold \(k\): the best candidate is at position \(i\) with probability \(\tfrac16\). She is hired exactly when \(i > k\) and the best among the first \(i - 1\) is among the first \(k\) (so nobody in positions \(k+1, \dots, i-1\) triggered a hire). That has probability \(\tfrac{k}{i-1}\).Why: the best of the first \(i - 1\) is equally likely to be at any of those \(i - 1\) positions.
  3. \(P(k) = \displaystyle\sum_{i=k+1}^{6}\frac16\cdot\frac{k}{i-1}\).Why: add over \(i\).
  4. \(P(1) = \tfrac16\big(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac15\big) = \tfrac{137}{360} \approx .381\); \(P(2) = \tfrac26\big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac15\big) = \tfrac{77}{180} \approx .428\); \(P(3) = \tfrac36\big(\tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac15\big) = \tfrac{47}{120} \approx .392\); \(P(4) = \tfrac46\big(\tfrac14 + \tfrac15\big) = .3\); \(P(5) = \tfrac56\cdot\tfrac15 = \tfrac16\).Why: evaluate step 3.
  5. The best is to skip 2: \(P = \) \(\tfrac{77}{180} \approx 0.428\). (For large \(n\): skip \(n/e\), giving \(\approx 1/e\).)Why: the largest value in step 4.

Conclusion. Skip 2 candidates, then take the first best-so-far: success probability \(\tfrac{77}{180}\). ∎

⚠ Course asserts the threshold form without justifying it (step 1). It also contains a typo: “\(P(5) = \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac16\)”.

Paid redraws from 1..100

$1 per draw (with replacement). You keep the last number drawn. \(\mathbb{E}[\text{net}]\) under optimal play?

Key idea

Draws are i.i.d. and the history doesn't matter, so the optimal rule is a threshold \(x\): stop at the first draw \(\ge x\).

Solution

Setup. Each draw costs $1 and gives a uniform number from 1–100 (with replacement). You may stop after any draw and keep the last number. Net = last number − number of draws.

  1. The optimal rule is a threshold \(x\): stop at the first draw \(\ge x\).Why: draws are i.i.d. and the history doesn't change the future, so the decision depends only on the current draw, and a larger draw is always at least as good to keep.
  2. With threshold \(x\), each draw succeeds with probability \(p = \tfrac{101 - x}{100}\), so the number of draws is geometric with mean \(\tfrac{100}{101 - x}\). The kept number is uniform on \(\{x, \dots, 100\}\), with mean \(\tfrac{x + 100}{2}\).Why: \(101 - x\) of the 100 values are \(\ge x\).
  3. \(E(x) = \dfrac{x + 100}{2} - \dfrac{100}{101 - x}\).Why: linearity: mean kept value minus mean cost.
  4. Setting \(E'(x) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac{100}{(101 - x)^2} = 0\) gives \((101 - x)^2 = 200\), so \(x \approx 101 - 14.14 = 86.9\).Why: calculus, to locate the best integers.
  5. \(E(86) = 93 - \tfrac{100}{15} \approx 86.333\), \(E(87) = 93.5 - \tfrac{100}{14} \approx 86.357\), \(E(88) = 94 - \tfrac{100}{13} \approx 86.308\). So \(x^* = 87\) and \(E = \) \(\approx\) $86.36.Why: compare the integer neighbours.
  6. Consistency: with value \(V \approx 86.36\) from continuing, you should stop exactly when the draw is \(\ge V\), i.e. \(\ge 87\) ✓.Why: stop iff the current draw beats the value of going on.

Conclusion. Stop at the first draw of 87 or more; the game is worth about $86.36. ∎

4 · Dynamic programming

State = everything that decides the future. \(V(\text{state}) = \max_{\text{action}} \mathbb{E}[\text{reward} + V(\text{next state})]\). Fill the table from the terminal states.

Guess the colour of each draw (3W, 3B)

$1 per correct guess. Optimal expected total?

Key idea

Guess the majority colour of what remains. The next state doesn't depend on your guess, so only this card's \(P(\text{correct})\) is being maximised.

Solution

Setup. 3 white and 3 black cards are dealt one at a time; before each card you guess its colour and win $1 if right.

  1. Level: \((w, b)\) = whites and blacks still in the deck; rec\((w, b)\) = the expected number of correct guesses from here.Why: only the remaining counts matter.
  2. Choice: guess W or B. The next state doesn't depend on the guess (the card is drawn anyway), so the best guess is the one more likely to be right: the majority colour, correct with probability \(\tfrac{\max(w,b)}{w+b}\).Why: your guess affects only this card's reward.
  3. Check: if \(w = 0\) or \(b = 0\), every remaining guess is right: \(w + b\).Why: you know every remaining colour.
  4. Move: \(\text{rec}(w,b) = \dfrac{\max(w,b)}{w+b} + \dfrac{w}{w+b}\text{rec}(w-1,b) + \dfrac{b}{w+b}\text{rec}(w,b-1)\).Why: reward now, plus the expected future over which colour is drawn.
double rec(ll w, ll b) {
    if (w == 0 || b == 0) return w + b;
    double n = w + b;
    return max(w, b) / n + w / n * rec(w - 1, b) + b / n * rec(w, b - 1);
}   // rec(3, 3)

+1 red, −1 black, quit any time (4R, 4B)

Key idea

State \((b,r)\) = cards remaining. The current total is \(b - r\). Take the max of quitting and drawing.

Solution

Setup. A deck of 4 red and 4 black cards. Each card drawn gives +1 if red and −1 if black; you may stop at any time and keep your total.

  1. Level: \((b, r)\) = blacks and reds left. The total so far is (reds drawn) − (blacks drawn) \(= (4 - r) - (4 - b) = b - r\). rec\((b, r)\) = the best expected final total.Why: the state determines the total.
  2. Choice: quit (keep \(b - r\)) or draw.Why: the rules.
  3. Check: \(b = 0\): only reds are left; drawing them all adds \(+r\) and ends at \(-r + r = 0\), which beats quitting at \(-r\). So rec = 0. \(r = 0\): only blacks are left, drawing only loses, so quit: rec = \(b\).Why: compare the options directly.
  4. Move: \(\text{rec}(b,r) = \max\Big\{b - r,\ \dfrac{b}{b+r}\text{rec}(b-1,r) + \dfrac{r}{b+r}\text{rec}(b,r-1)\Big\}\).Why: a black is drawn with probability \(\tfrac{b}{b+r}\), a red with \(\tfrac{r}{b+r}\).
double rec(ll b, ll r) {
    if (b == 0) return 0;
    if (r == 0) return b;
    double n = b + r;
    return max((double)(b - r), b / n * rec(b - 1, r) + r / n * rec(b, r - 1));
}   // rec(4, 4)

5 · Lessons from the past

Symmetry, small cases and induction still work. For guessing games, choose the best answer separately for each observation: that maximises the total probability of being right.

Is \(X_1\) the min, the median or the max?

Three i.i.d. \(U[0,1]\). You see only \(X_1 = x\).

Key idea

Given \(x\): \(P(\text{min}) = (1-x)^2,\ P(\text{median}) = 2x(1-x),\ P(\text{max}) = x^2\). Pick the largest.

Solution

Setup. \(X_1, X_2, X_3\) are independent uniforms on \([0, 1]\). You see only \(X_1 = x\) and guess whether it is the min, the median or the max.

  1. Given \(x\): \(P(\text{min}) = (1 - x)^2\), \(P(\text{max}) = x^2\), \(P(\text{median}) = 2x(1 - x)\).Why: min means both others are above \(x\); max means both below; median means one below and one above (2 orders).
  2. \((1 - x)^2 \ge 2x(1 - x) \iff 1 - x \ge 2x \iff x \le \tfrac13\), and \(x^2 \ge 2x(1 - x) \iff x \ge 2(1 - x) \iff x \ge \tfrac23\).Why: divide by \(1 - x > 0\), resp. \(x > 0\).
  3. Rule: guess min if \(x \le \tfrac13\), median if \(\tfrac13 < x < \tfrac23\), max if \(x \ge \tfrac23\). It is optimal because it picks the most likely answer for every single \(x\).Why: total success probability = average over \(x\) of the chosen answer's probability.
  4. \(P = \displaystyle\int_0^{1/3}(1-x)^2dx + \int_{1/3}^{2/3}2x(1-x)\,dx + \int_{2/3}^{1}x^2dx\). The first and last are equal by symmetry, each \(\tfrac{1 - (2/3)^3}{3} = \tfrac{19}{81}\); the middle is \(\big[x^2 - \tfrac{2x^3}{3}\big]_{1/3}^{2/3} = \tfrac{20}{81} - \tfrac{7}{81} = \tfrac{13}{81}\).Why: integrate each piece.
  5. \(P = \tfrac{38}{81} + \tfrac{13}{81} = \tfrac{51}{81} = \) \(\tfrac{17}{27} \approx 0.63\).Why: add.

Conclusion. The best rule is right with probability \(\tfrac{17}{27}\). ∎

⚠ Course optimises only over symmetric thresholds \((\alpha, 1-\alpha)\) and assumes the optimum lies in that family. The comparison for each \(x\) (steps 2–3) proves it.

Alternate guessing a secret in 1..100

After each wrong guess, both players hear "higher" or "lower". You go first. \(P(\text{you win})\)?

Key idea

Let \(W_n = n\,p_n\) (the expected number of secrets the first player wins). Then \(W_n = \max_k \big[n - W_{k-1} - W_{n-k}\big]\), and the solution is \(W_n = \lceil n/2 \rceil\).

Solution

Setup. A secret is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\). Players alternate guesses; after each wrong guess both hear 'higher' or 'lower'. \(W_n\) = the number of the \(n\) possible secrets that the player about to guess wins (so their win probability is \(p_n = W_n / n\)).

  1. Level: rec\((n) = W_n\). Choice: guess \(k\). Check: \(n = 0 \Rightarrow 0\).Why: only the size of the remaining range matters.
  2. Move: if the secret is \(k\), you win (1 secret). If it is among the \(k - 1\) below, the opponent now guesses in a range of \(k - 1\) and wins \(W_{k-1}\) of those secrets, so you win \((k - 1) - W_{k-1}\). Likewise above: \((n - k) - W_{n-k}\). So \(\text{rec}(n) = \max_k\big[1 + (k-1) - W_{k-1} + (n-k) - W_{n-k}\big] = \max_k\big[n - W_{k-1} - W_{n-k}\big]\).Why: the new range is uniform over the remaining values.
  3. Claim: \(W_n = \lceil n/2 \rceil\). Induction: assume it for all \(m < n\), and write \(a = k - 1\), \(b = n - k\), so \(a + b = n - 1\).Why: strong induction; \(n = 0\) gives 0 ✓.
  4. \(n\) even: \(a + b\) is odd, so one of \(a, b\) is even and the other odd, and \(\lceil a/2\rceil + \lceil b/2\rceil = \tfrac{a + b + 1}{2} = \tfrac n2\) for every \(k\). So \(W_n = n - \tfrac n2 = \tfrac n2\).Why: the ceiling adds \(\tfrac12\) to the odd one only.
  5. \(n\) odd: \(a + b\) is even. If both are even, the ceilings sum to \(\tfrac{n-1}{2}\), giving \(n - \tfrac{n-1}{2} = \tfrac{n+1}{2}\) (e.g. \(k = 1\): \(a = 0\)). If both are odd, they sum to \(\tfrac{n+1}{2}\), giving \(\tfrac{n-1}{2}\). The max is \(W_n = \tfrac{n+1}{2}\).Why: both parities are possible; take the better.
  6. So \(W_n = \lceil n/2\rceil\), and \(p_{100} = \tfrac{50}{100} = \) \(\tfrac12\).Why: steps 4–5. (DP-checked for \(n \le 100\).)

Conclusion. With 100 numbers, going first wins with probability exactly \(\tfrac12\). ∎

ll rec(ll n) {
    if (n == 0) return 0;
    ll ans = 0;
    for (ll k = 1; k <= n; k++) ans = max(ans, n - rec(k - 1) - rec(n - k));
    return ans;
}   // rec(100) / 100

⚠ Course's induction ends with “a similar check yields \(\tfrac12\)”. The substitution \(W_n = n\,p_n\) (steps 2–5) makes every case a one-line parity count.
