A · Fundamentals (8 × 1 mark)

Before A1 · The uniform distribution

Picture. Throw a dart at a segment [a, b] so that no spot is favoured. That landing point X is Uniform[a, b].

  • Density is flat: \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{b-a}\) on [a, b], 0 outside. Flat means "every spot equally likely".
  • Probability = length share: \(P(c \le X \le d) = \tfrac{d-c}{b-a}\). For Uniform[0, 1] this is just the length d − c. This is the one fact you use constantly.
  • CDF is a straight line: \(P(X \le x) = x\) on [0, 1]. Conversely, any variable whose CDF is a straight line on an interval is uniform there.
  • Mean = midpoint \(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\) (balance point of a flat bar). Variance \(\tfrac{(b-a)^2}{12}\); for [0, 1]: E[X²] = ∫x² dx = 1/3, so Var = 1/3 − 1/4 = 1/12.
  • Symmetry: if U ~ Uniform[0, 1], so is 1 − U (flip the segment).

Method for any function of U (like a max or a "longer piece"): compute \(P(\text{thing} \le x)\) as a length, then read off the distribution. If that CDF turns out linear, the thing is itself uniform.

A1 · Snapped Ruler 1 mark

A 1-metre ruler is snapped at a uniformly random point. You keep the longer piece.

Question: what is its expected length?

Solution

Key idea

max(U, 1 − U) is itself uniform on [½, 1].

Picture

Fold the ruler at the middle. The snap point folds onto a uniform point of [½, 1]; that is the longer piece.

Solution

Setup. U ~ Uniform[0, 1] is the snap point; L = max(U, 1 − U).

Claim. 3/4 m.

  1. For ½ ≤ x ≤ 1: \(P(L \le x) = P(1 - x \le U \le x) = 2x - 1\).Why: both pieces ≤ x iff U is within x of each end.
  2. So L ~ Uniform[½, 1] and \(E[L] = \tfrac34\).Why: the CDF 2x − 1 is linear on [½, 1].

Answer. 3/4 m. ∎

Before A2 · The Poisson process and the exponential distribution (also used in A3, A7, B2, C2)

Picture. Events (buses, customers, toggles) are dots on a time line, dropped "completely at random" at an average rate of λ per unit time.

Definition. A Poisson process of rate λ is a random set of times with:

  1. Independent increments: counts in non-overlapping time intervals are independent.
  2. Poisson counts: the number N(s, s+t] of events in any interval of length t is Poisson(λt): \(P(N = k) = e^{-\lambda t}\tfrac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!}\), mean λt, variance λt.

Where the Poisson count comes from. Chop [0, t] into n tiny slots, each holding an event with chance λt/n, independently. The count is Binomial(n, λt/n) → Poisson(λt) as n → ∞. So "many tiny independent chances" = Poisson.

Link to the exponential. Let T₁ be the time of the first event. Then T₁ > t ⟺ no event in [0, t], so

\(P(T_1 > t) = P(N(0, t] = 0) = e^{-\lambda t}\), i.e. \(T_1 \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)\): density \(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\), mean \(1/\lambda\), variance \(1/\lambda^2\).

By independent increments the process restarts after every event, so the gaps \(T_1, T_2 - T_1, T_3 - T_2, …\) are i.i.d. Exp(λ). Conversely, i.i.d. Exp(λ) gaps build a Poisson process. Poisson counts ⟺ exponential gaps: two descriptions of the same dots.

Memoryless. \(P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = \tfrac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(T > t)\). However long you have waited, the remaining wait is still Exp(λ). The exponential is the only continuous distribution with this property.

Toolkit used below.

  • k-th event: \(T_k\) = sum of k gaps, mean k/λ, variance k/λ² (Gamma(k, λ)).
  • Merging: independent processes of rates λ₁, λ₂ together form a Poisson process of rate λ₁ + λ₂; the next event is from the first with chance λ₁/(λ₁ + λ₂).
  • Thinning (A3, C2): keep each event independently with chance p → kept and dropped events are independent Poisson processes of rates pλ and (1 − p)λ.
  • Conditioning (B2): given exactly n events in [0, T], their times are n i.i.d. Uniform[0, T] points, sorted.
  • Time reversal (A2): looking backward from any fixed time, the time since the last event is also Exp(λ), independent of the future.

A2 · The Bus You Just Missed 1 mark

Buses arrive at a stop at completely random times (independent of each other and of the past), on average one every 10 minutes. You turn up at a random moment.

Question: what is the expected time between the bus that came just before you and the bus you catch?

Solution

Key idea

"Completely random" = Poisson process, rate 1/10. Looking forward and backward from your arrival, both waits are Exp(mean 10) and independent: 20, not 10 (inspection paradox).

Picture

You are more likely to land in a long gap. A gap twice as long catches you twice as often.

Solution

Setup. Bus times form a Poisson process of rate λ = 1/10 per minute. You arrive at time t. F = time to the next bus, B = time since the last bus.

Claim. 20 min.

  1. F ~ Exp(1/10), E[F] = 10.Why: memoryless: the future after t is a fresh Poisson process.
  2. B ~ Exp(1/10) (for t large), E[B] = 10, independent of F.Why: a Poisson process run backwards in time is again a Poisson process of the same rate; past and future counts are independent.
  3. E[B + F] = 20.Why: linearity. Check: length-biased gap mean \(E[G^2]/E[G] = 2\cdot 10^2/10 = 20\).

Answer. 20 min. ∎

Before A3 · The Poisson distribution and thinning

Picture. Count how many things happen in a fixed window when each of a huge number of tiny moments independently has a tiny chance of an event: customers in an hour, typos on a page, goals in a match. That count N is Poisson(λ), where λ = the average count.

  • PMF: \(P(N = k) = e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}\), k = 0, 1, 2, … It is the limit of Binomial(n, λ/n) as n → ∞: many trials, each rarely succeeds.
  • Mean = variance = λ. (Binomial variance np(1 − p) → np when p is tiny.) So a Poisson count of mean 9 typically wobbles by about √9 = 3.
  • Sums: independent Poisson(λ₁) + Poisson(λ₂) = Poisson(λ₁ + λ₂). Two independent streams of rare events pooled together are still rare events.

Thinning (the key property here). Take a Poisson(λ) count and flip an independent p-coin for each event: "kept" or "dropped". Then

  • kept ~ Poisson(pλ), dropped ~ Poisson((1 − p)λ), and they are independent.
  • Why this is surprising: if the total were a fixed number, say 10, then more kept means fewer dropped: strongly dependent. With a Poisson total, the total itself is random, and the two effects cancel exactly. Intuition: in the tiny-slot picture, each slot independently holds a kept event (chance pλ/n), a dropped one ((1 − p)λ/n), or nothing. Kept events and dropped events live in separate slots, so they are two separate rare-event counts.
  • Consequence: learning one of them tells you nothing about the other.

A3 · Browsers and Buyers 1 mark

Customers walk into a shop at random, 10 per hour on average (Poisson). Each customer independently buys something with probability 0.3; the rest just browse. In a particular hour exactly 5 customers bought something.

Question: what is the expected number of browsers in that hour?

Solution

Key idea

Thinning: buyers and browsers are independent Poisson processes (rates 3 and 7). Knowing the buyers says nothing about the browsers.

Picture

The trap answer is 5·(7/3) ≈ 11.7. That would be right if the total were fixed; here it is random, and the two streams split apart independently.

Solution

Setup. N ~ Poisson(10) customers; B = buyers, R = browsers, B + R = N.

Claim. 7.

  1. \(P(B = b, R = r) = e^{-10}\tfrac{10^{b+r}}{(b+r)!}\binom{b+r}{b}0.3^b0.7^r = e^{-3}\tfrac{3^b}{b!}\cdot e^{-7}\tfrac{7^r}{r!}\).Why: choose N = b + r, then which b of them buy; \(10^{b+r}0.3^b0.7^r = 3^b7^r\).
  2. The joint law factors, so B ~ Poisson(3) and R ~ Poisson(7) are independent.Why: a product of a function of b and a function of r.
  3. \(E[R \mid B = 5] = E[R] = 7\).Why: independence.

Answer. 7. ∎

Before A4 · The normal distribution

Picture. The bell curve. It appears whenever a quantity is the sum of many small independent effects (heights: many genes and nutrition factors; price moves: many trades). That is the Central Limit Theorem.

  • Parameters: X ~ N(μ, σ²) has mean μ (centre of the bell) and variance σ² (σ = standard deviation = width). Density \(\tfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\).
  • 68–95–99.7 rule: within 1σ of the mean about 68% of the time, within 2σ about 95%, within 3σ about 99.7%.
  • Standardising: \(Z = \tfrac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\) ~ N(0, 1). Every normal question becomes "how many σ away is this?". \(\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z)\) is the standard normal CDF.
  • Symmetry: the bell is symmetric about 0, so \(P(Z > z) = P(Z < -z) = \Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)\).
  • Adding independent normals gives a normal: if X ~ N(μ₁, σ₁²) and Y ~ N(μ₂, σ₂²) are independent, then X ± Y ~ N(μ₁ ± μ₂, σ₁² + σ₂²).
  • Variances add even for a difference. Subtracting a random thing adds uncertainty, never removes it: Var(X − Y) = Var X + Var(−Y) = Var X + Var Y. Standard deviations do not add: σ = √(σ₁² + σ₂²) (Pythagoras).

A4 · Taller Partner 1 mark

Men's heights are N(170, 6²) cm and women's heights are N(160, 8²) cm. A man and a woman are picked independently at random.

Question: what is the probability that the woman is taller? (Answer in Φ.)

Solution

Key idea

A difference of independent normals is normal: means subtract, variances add.

Picture

W − M ~ N(−10, 10²). The woman is taller exactly when this is above 0: one SD above the mean.

Solution

Claim. Φ(−1) ≈ 0.1587.

  1. \(W - M \sim N(160 - 170,\ 8^2 + 6^2) = N(-10, 100)\).Why: independent normals; Var(W − M) = Var W + Var M.
  2. \(P(W - M > 0) = P\big(Z > \tfrac{0 - (-10)}{10}\big) = P(Z > 1) = \Phi(-1)\).Why: standardise; symmetry of Z.

Answer. Φ(−1) ≈ 0.1587. ∎

Before A5 · Two independent uniforms, and the tail formula

  • Two independent Uniform[0, 1] points (X, Y) = one dart thrown uniformly at the unit square. So probability = area of the region where the event happens. Draw the square, shade the region, compute the area.
  • Example: {X < Y} is the triangle above the diagonal, area ½. {|X − Y| > t} is two corner triangles away from the diagonal.
  • Tail formula for expectations: for a variable W ≥ 0, \(E[W] = \int_0^\infty P(W > t)\,dt\). Intuition: slice W's value into thin layers dt; the layer at height t is "counted" exactly when W > t. So the average of W = sum of the chances of exceeding each level.
  • Gap lemma: n independent uniform points cut [0, 1] into n + 1 pieces. By symmetry no piece is special, and the pieces add up to 1, so each has mean \(\tfrac{1}{n+1}\).

A5 · Lunch Date 1 mark

Two friends each arrive at a café at an independent, uniformly random time between 12:00 and 13:00. The first to arrive waits for the other.

Question: how long, on average, does the first one wait?

Solution

Key idea

E|X − Y| for two uniforms on [0, 1] is 1/3: two random points cut [0, 1] into 3 pieces of equal mean.

Picture

Three gaps, mean ⅓ each. Before the first, between them, after the second; the wait is the middle one.

Solution

Setup. X, Y ~ Uniform[0, 1] (in hours), independent. Wait = |X − Y|.

Claim. 20 min.

  1. \(P(|X - Y| > t) = (1 - t)^2\) for 0 ≤ t ≤ 1.Why: two corner triangles of the unit square, legs 1 − t, total area \((1-t)^2\).
  2. \(E|X - Y| = \int_0^1 (1-t)^2\,dt = \tfrac13\) hour.Why: E[W] = ∫ P(W > t) dt for W ≥ 0.

Answer. 1/3 hour = 20 min. ∎

Before A6 · Moments of the normal distribution

The k-th moment of X is E[X^k]. For Z ~ N(0, 1):

  • Odd moments are 0: E[Z] = E[Z³] = … = 0. The bell is symmetric, so every positive value z is balanced by −z, and z^k cancels (−z)^k when k is odd.
  • E[Z²] = 1: that is what variance 1 means (mean is 0).
  • E[Z⁴] = 3. Trick (Stein's identity): \(E[Z\,g(Z)] = E[g'(Z)]\), which follows from integration by parts because the density's derivative is \(-z\) times the density. Take g(z) = z³: E[Z⁴] = E[3Z²] = 3. Continuing: E[Z⁶] = 5·3 = 15, E[Z^{2m}] = 1·3·5⋯(2m − 1).
  • Scaling: X ~ N(0, σ²) is X = σZ, so E[X^k] = σ^k E[Z^k]. E[X²] = σ², E[X⁴] = 3σ⁴.
  • Why 3 matters: E[X⁴]/σ⁴ is the "kurtosis", a measure of tail heaviness. The normal's is 3; real returns usually exceed 3 (fat tails).
  • Variance of anything: Var(W) = E[W²] − (E[W])². For W = X², that needs the 4th moment.

A6 · Squared Move 1 mark

Tomorrow's price move X has mean 0 and standard deviation 2, and is normally distributed. A contract pays X² (a variance swap in miniature).

Question: what is the variance of the payoff?

Solution

Key idea

Normal fourth moment: \(E[X^4] = 3\sigma^4\). So Var(X²) = 3σ⁴ − σ⁴ = 2σ⁴.

Picture

Mean 4, but big tails. The payoff is 4 on average, and its spread is √32 ≈ 5.7, larger than the mean.

Solution

Setup. X = 2Z with Z ~ N(0, 1).

Claim. 32.

  1. \(E[Z^4] = 3\).Why: integrate by parts: \(E[Z^4] = E[Z^3\cdot Z] = 3E[Z^2] = 3\) (Stein: \(E[Zg(Z)] = E[g'(Z)]\)).
  2. \(E[X^2] = 4\), \(E[X^4] = 16\cdot 3 = 48\).Why: X = 2Z.
  3. \(\text{Var}(X^2) = 48 - 4^2 = 32\).Why: Var = E[(X²)²] − (E[X²])².

Answer. 32. ∎

Before A7 · Poisson counts and the exponential series

  • "Toggles at completely random moments, on average λ per minute" = Poisson process; the number of toggles in t minutes is Poisson(λt) (see the primer before A2).
  • The Poisson probabilities are just the terms of the exponential series: \(e^{\lambda} = \sum_k \tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}\), so \(\sum_k e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!} = 1\).
  • Generating function: \(E[s^N] = \sum_k e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{(\lambda s)^k}{k!} = e^{\lambda(s-1)}\). Plugging in special values of s answers questions for free.
  • s = −1 detects parity: \((-1)^N\) is +1 when N is even and −1 when odd. So \(E[(-1)^N] = P(\text{even}) - P(\text{odd})\).

A7 · Flickering Switch 1 mark

A light starts off. A faulty relay toggles it at random moments, on average once a minute, the moments being completely random (Poisson).

Question: what is the probability that the light is off after 3 minutes?

Solution

Key idea

Off ⟺ an even number of toggles. For N ~ Poisson(λ): \(P(\text{even}) = \tfrac{1 + e^{-2\lambda}}{2}\).

Picture

Almost a fair coin. After 3 expected toggles, the parity has nearly forgotten where it started: the bias is only \(e^{-6}/2\).

Solution

Setup. N ~ Poisson(3) toggles.

Claim. \(\tfrac{1 + e^{-6}}{2}\).

  1. \(P(\text{even}) - P(\text{odd}) = \sum_n e^{-3}\tfrac{3^n}{n!}(-1)^n = e^{-3}e^{-3} = e^{-6}\).Why: the exponential series at x = −3.
  2. \(P(\text{even}) + P(\text{odd}) = 1\), so \(P(\text{even}) = \tfrac{1 + e^{-6}}{2}\).Why: add the two equations and halve.

Answer. (1 + e^{−6})/2 ≈ 0.50124. ∎

Before A8 · Order statistics of uniforms

  • Take n independent Uniform[0, 1] values and sort them: \(U_{(1)} < U_{(2)} < \dots < U_{(n)}\). \(U_{(k)}\) is the k-th smallest ("k-th order statistic"). \(U_{(n)}\) is the max, \(U_{(1)}\) the min.
  • Mean of the k-th smallest: \(E[U_{(k)}] = \tfrac{k}{n+1}\). Intuition: the n points cut [0, 1] into n + 1 gaps; the gaps are exchangeable (you could relabel them without changing anything) and add to 1, so each averages \(\tfrac1{n+1}\). The k-th point sits k gaps from 0.
  • Examples: max of 2 averages 2/3, min of 3 averages 1/4, median of 3 averages 1/2.
  • Scaling: for Uniform[0, M], multiply by M.
  • Max and min CDFs: \(P(U_{(n)} \le x) = x^n\) (all below x), \(P(U_{(1)} > x) = (1-x)^n\) (all above x).

A8 · Second-Price Auction 1 mark

Four bidders have independent values, each uniform on [0, 100]. In a second-price auction everyone bids their value; the highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid.

Question: what is the seller's expected revenue?

Solution

Key idea

n uniform points cut [0, 1] into n + 1 gaps of equal mean, so the k-th smallest has mean k/(n + 1).

Picture

Four bids, five equal gaps of mean 20. The second-highest is the 3rd smallest: 3 gaps up, 60.

Solution

Setup. Values \(100U_1, …, 100U_4\); \(U_{(k)}\) = k-th smallest.

Claim. 60.

  1. \(E[U_{(k)}] = \tfrac{k}{n+1}\).Why: the 5 gaps (with the ends) are exchangeable and add to 1, so each has mean 1/5.
  2. Revenue = \(100\,U_{(3)}\), mean \(100\cdot\tfrac35 = 60\).Why: second-highest of 4 = 3rd smallest.

Answer. 60. ∎

B · Standard tricks (4 × 2 marks)

Before B1 · Sums of uniforms, the tail formula and Wald's identity

  • P(U₁ + … + Uₙ ≤ 1) = 1/n!. The points \((u_1, …, u_n)\) in the unit cube with sum ≤ 1 form a corner "simplex": a triangle of area 1/2 for n = 2, a tetrahedron of volume 1/6 for n = 3, in general 1/n!. Probability = volume, because the n uniforms together are a uniform point in the cube.
  • Tail formula for a count: for N taking values 0, 1, 2, …, \(E[N] = \sum_{n \ge 0} P(N > n)\). (N is counted once for each level n below it.)
  • Wald's identity: if you add i.i.d. values X₁, X₂, … and the decision to stop after step k uses only X₁..X_k (no peeking ahead), then \(E[X_1 + \dots + X_N] = E[N]\cdot E[X]\).
  • Why it is not obvious: the last value you add tends to be large (a big one is what pushed you over), so you might expect a bias. But whether step k happens is decided before seeing X_k, so each added X_k is still an average draw.

B1 · Overfilled Bag 2 marks

Stones with independent weights, each uniform on [0, 1] kg, are dropped into a bag one at a time until the total weight first exceeds 1 kg.

Question: what is the expected final weight of the bag?

Solution

Key idea

Number of stones N has \(P(N > n) = 1/n!\), so E[N] = e. Wald's identity: E[total] = E[N]·E[stone] = e/2.

Picture

More stones than you think. On average e ≈ 2.72 stones, each averaging ½ kg even though the last one tends to be heavy.

Solution

Setup. \(S_n = U_1 + \dots + U_n\), N = min{n : S_n > 1}.

Claim. e/2 ≈ 1.359 kg.

  1. \(P(N > n) = P(S_n \le 1) = \tfrac1{n!}\).Why: \(\{S_n \le 1\}\) is the simplex \(x_i \ge 0, \sum x_i \le 1\), volume 1/n!.
  2. \(E[N] = \sum_{n\ge0} P(N > n) = \sum_{n \ge 0}\tfrac1{n!} = e\).Why: tail-sum formula for a count.
  3. \(E[S_N] = E[N]\cdot E[U] = \tfrac e2\).Why: Wald: whether stone k is added depends only on stones 1..k−1, so \(E[U_k 1_{N \ge k}] = \tfrac12 P(N \ge k)\); sum over k.

Answer. e/2 ≈ 1.359 kg. ∎

Before B2 · A Poisson process given its count: uniform points

  • Recall (primer before A2): the gaps between events are i.i.d. Exp(λ), and the count in [0, T] is Poisson(λT).
  • Key property: if you are told there were exactly n events in [0, T], then their times look like n independent Uniform[0, T] points, sorted. The rate λ disappears.
  • Why: the chance of the events landing near times \(t_1 < \dots < t_n\) is \(\lambda^n e^{-\lambda T}\,dt_1\cdots dt_n\): each event contributes λ dt, and the empty stretches together contribute \(e^{-\lambda(\text{total empty length})} = e^{-\lambda T}\) roughly. This doesn't depend on where the t's are, so every arrangement is equally likely: exactly the behaviour of uniform points.
  • Intuition: a Poisson process has no preferred moments. Once the number of events is fixed, the only thing left is "where", and nowhere is special.
  • Then use the uniform order statistics from A8: the k-th of n points on [0, T] sits at \(\tfrac{k}{n+1}T\) on average.

B2 · Three Buses in an Hour 2 marks

Buses arrive as a Poisson process (rate unknown). You are told that exactly 3 buses came between 9:00 and 10:00.

Question: what is the expected time of the first of them?

Solution

Key idea

Given n arrivals in [0, T], the arrival times are n i.i.d. uniform points on [0, T] (sorted). The rate drops out.

Picture

Three uniform points, four equal gaps of 15 min. The first bus is one gap in: 9:15.

Solution

Setup. Arrival times \(T_1 < T_2 < T_3\) in [0, 1] hour, rate λ.

Claim. 9:15.

  1. The density of \((T_1, T_2, T_3) = (t_1, t_2, t_3)\) with exactly 3 arrivals is \(\lambda e^{-\lambda t_1}\cdot\lambda e^{-\lambda(t_2 - t_1)}\cdot\lambda e^{-\lambda(t_3 - t_2)}\cdot e^{-\lambda(1 - t_3)} = \lambda^3 e^{-\lambda}\).Why: three Exp(λ) gaps, then no arrival in the rest.
  2. This is constant on \(t_1 < t_2 < t_3\), so the times are sorted i.i.d. uniforms.Why: a flat density on the ordered region is exactly the law of sorted uniforms.
  3. \(E[T_1] = \tfrac1{3+1}\) hour = 15 min.Why: min of 3 uniforms, gap lemma (A8).

Answer. 9:15. ∎

Before B3 · Absolute value of a normal, and max as an average

  • Half-normal: for Z ~ N(0, 1), |Z| folds the bell onto [0, ∞). Its mean is \(E|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.80\): a typical distance from the centre is 0.8σ, a bit less than σ.
  • Derivation: \(E|Z| = 2\int_0^\infty z\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}dz = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\big[-e^{-z^2/2}\big]_0^\infty = \sqrt{2/\pi}\). The z in front is exactly the derivative of the exponent, so the integral is immediate.
  • Scaling: for W ~ N(0, s²), \(E|W| = s\sqrt{2/\pi}\).
  • Difference of independent normals (from A4): X − Y ~ N(μ₁ − μ₂, σ₁² + σ₂²).
  • Max identity (any two numbers): \(\max(x, y) = \tfrac{x+y}{2} + \tfrac{|x-y|}{2}\): start at the midpoint, go up half the gap. Taking expectations turns a max into an absolute value, which the half-normal handles.

B3 · Hire the Better Trader 2 marks

Two traders' daily PnLs are independent, each normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 10. You get paid the larger of the two PnLs.

Question: what is your expected pay?

Solution

Key idea

max(X, Y) = (X + Y)/2 + |X − Y|/2, and for W ~ N(0, s²), \(E|W| = s\sqrt{2/\pi}\).

Picture

The value of choice. Each alone is worth 0; picking the better one is worth σ/√π ≈ 0.56σ.

Solution

Setup. X, Y ~ N(0, 100) independent.

Claim. \(10/\sqrt\pi \approx 5.642\).

  1. \(\max(X, Y) = \tfrac{X + Y}{2} + \tfrac{|X - Y|}{2}\).Why: the average plus half the gap.
  2. \(X - Y \sim N(0, 200)\), so \(E|X - Y| = \sqrt{200}\sqrt{2/\pi} = \tfrac{20}{\sqrt\pi}\).Why: \(E|Z| = 2\int_0^\infty \tfrac{z}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}dz = \sqrt{2/\pi}\).
  3. \(E[\max] = 0 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{20}{\sqrt\pi} = \tfrac{10}{\sqrt\pi}\).Why: linearity; E[X + Y] = 0.

Answer. 10/√π ≈ 5.642. ∎

Before B4 · Uniform spacings and inclusion–exclusion

  • Spacings: two uniform cuts split [0, 1] into three pieces \((S_1, S_2, S_3)\) with \(S_1 + S_2 + S_3 = 1\). They are uniformly spread over all ways to split 1 into three non-negative parts (a triangle, the "simplex"), and exchangeable: the left, middle and right piece have the same distribution.
  • Shrink trick: "every piece in a chosen set K is longer than x" — reserve x of length for each piece in K; what is left is a random split of length 1 − |K|x. The triangle shrinks by the factor 1 − |K|x in each of its 2 dimensions, so the chance is \((1 - |K|x)^2\) (when that is positive).
  • Inclusion–exclusion: \(P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3) = \sum P(A_i) - \sum P(A_i A_j) + P(A_1A_2A_3)\). "Biggest piece > x" is exactly "some piece > x".
  • Tail formula (from A5): E[M] = ∫₀¹ P(M > x) dx.

B4 · Biggest Chunk 2 marks

A 1-metre chocolate bar is snapped at two independent, uniformly random points. You take the biggest piece.

Question: what is its expected length?

Solution

Key idea

Inclusion–exclusion on "piece i is longer than x": for 2 uniform cuts, any k given pieces all exceed x with probability \((1 - kx)^2\).

Picture

Shrink trick. Forcing k pieces to be at least x each removes kx of length; what is left is again two uniform cuts on a bar of length 1 − kx.

Solution

Setup. Pieces \(S_1, S_2, S_3\) (left, middle, right); M = max.

Claim. 11/18 m.

  1. \(P(S_i > x \text{ for all } i \in K) = (1 - |K|x)_+^2\).Why: the spacings are uniform on the simplex \(s_1 + s_2 + s_3 = 1\); the region \(s_i > x\) (i ∈ K) is a copy scaled by \(1 - |K|x\), and the simplex is 2-dimensional.
  2. \(P(M > x) = 3(1-x)^2 - 3(1-2x)_+^2 + (1-3x)_+^2\).Why: inclusion–exclusion over the 3 events \(S_i > x\).
  3. \(E[M] = \int_0^1 P(M > x)\,dx = 3\cdot\tfrac13 - 3\cdot\tfrac16 + \tfrac19 = \tfrac{11}{18}\).Why: \(\int_0^{1/k}(1 - kx)^2dx = \tfrac1{3k}\).

Answer. 11/18 m ≈ 0.611 m. ∎

Before C1 · Correlated normals (bivariate normal)

  • Correlation ρ ∈ [−1, 1] measures how much two quantities move together: \(\rho = \tfrac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\). ρ = 1: locked together, ρ = 0: unrelated, ρ = −1: mirror images.
  • Jointly normal with correlation ρ (both N(0, 1)) can always be built as \(Y = \rho X + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,Z\) with X, Z independent N(0, 1): Y is "ρ parts X plus fresh noise". Check: Var Y = ρ² + (1 − ρ²) = 1, Cov(X, Y) = ρ.
  • A pair of jointly normal variables is completely determined by their means, variances and correlation: match those and you have the same distribution.
  • Rotation invariance: two independent N(0, 1) values (Z₁, Z₂) form a random point in the plane whose density \(\tfrac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}\) depends only on the distance from the origin. So the direction of the point is uniform around the circle: a perfectly round cloud.
  • Geometry of dot products: for unit vectors a, b: a·b = cos(angle between them). "a·g > 0" is a half-plane through the origin.

C1 · Both Stocks Up 4 marks

Two stocks' daily returns are jointly normal, each with mean 0, and their correlation is 0.5.

Question: what is the probability that both stocks go up tomorrow?

Solution

Key idea

Write both returns as projections of one rotation-invariant 2-D normal vector. "Both up" is a wedge; rotation invariance turns the probability into (wedge angle)/2π. General answer \(\tfrac14 + \tfrac{\arcsin\rho}{2\pi}\).

Picture

Two half-planes at 60°. The directions a and b have angle arccos 0.5 = 60° between them; the region where both dot products are positive is a wedge of 180° − 60° = 120°, a third of the circle.

Solution

Setup. Scale each return to variance 1. Take \(G = (Z_1, Z_2)\) i.i.d. N(0, 1); set \(X = a\cdot G\), \(Y = b\cdot G\) with unit vectors a, b, \(a\cdot b = \rho = \tfrac12\).

Claim. 1/3.

  1. (X, Y) has the right law.Why: jointly normal, mean 0, Var X = |a|² = 1, Var Y = 1, Cov(X, Y) = a·b = ρ; a bivariate normal is fixed by these.
  2. The direction of G is uniform on the circle.Why: its density \(\tfrac1{2\pi}e^{-|g|^2/2}\) depends only on |g|.
  3. {X > 0, Y > 0} = {G in the wedge where a·g > 0 and b·g > 0}, of angle π − θ with θ = arccos ρ = π/3.Why: each condition is a half-plane; two half-planes whose normals are θ apart meet in a wedge of angle π − θ.
  4. \(P = \tfrac{\pi - \pi/3}{2\pi} = \tfrac13\).Why: uniform direction; the length of G does not matter. (General: \(\tfrac{\pi - \arccos\rho}{2\pi} = \tfrac14 + \tfrac{\arcsin\rho}{2\pi}\).)

Answer. 1/3. ∎

Before C2 · Poissonisation: splitting a Poisson count

  • This is thinning (A3) with more than two bins. Take N ~ Poisson(λ) items and throw each independently into one of m bins with probabilities p₁, …, p_m.
  • Property: the bin counts are independent, with bin j ~ Poisson(p_j λ).
  • Contrast with a fixed total: with exactly n items, bin counts are multinomial and dependent (they must add to n; a full bin means fewer items elsewhere). Randomising the total with a Poisson removes that constraint, which is why this is called "Poissonisation": a standard trick to make occupancy problems (balls in bins, coupon collecting) easy.
  • Empty bin: a Poisson(μ) count is 0 with probability \(e^{-\mu}\), so a bin is non-empty with probability \(1 - e^{-\mu}\).

C2 · Touch Every Stock 4 marks

A trader makes a random number of trades in a day: Poisson with mean 6. Each trade is in one of 6 stocks, chosen uniformly and independently.

Question: what is the probability that every stock is traded at least once?

Solution

Key idea

Poissonisation: splitting a Poisson(6) count 6 ways gives 6 independent Poisson(1) counts. Then "all ≥ 1" just multiplies. (With a fixed 6 trades it would be 6!/6⁶ ≈ 0.015; the random count makes it clean.)

Picture

Each stock is its own Poisson(1) stream. Each is non-empty with chance 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632, independently: 0.632⁶ ≈ 0.064.

Solution

Setup. N ~ Poisson(6) trades; \(N_j\) = trades in stock j, \(N_1 + \dots + N_6 = N\).

Claim. \((1 - e^{-1})^6 \approx 0.0637\).

  1. \(P(N_1 = n_1, …, N_6 = n_6) = e^{-6}\tfrac{6^n}{n!}\cdot\tfrac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_6!}6^{-n} = \prod_{j=1}^6 e^{-1}\tfrac{1}{n_j!}\).Why: choose N = n = Σ n_j, then a multinomial split; \(6^n\) cancels.
  2. So \(N_1, …, N_6\) are independent Poisson(1).Why: the joint law is a product of Poisson(1) pmfs.
  3. \(P(\text{all } N_j \ge 1) = (1 - e^{-1})^6\).Why: independence; \(P(N_j = 0) = e^{-1}\).

Answer. (1 − 1/e)⁶ ≈ 0.0637. ∎
