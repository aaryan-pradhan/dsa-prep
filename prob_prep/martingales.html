1 · Stochastic processes

A stochastic process is a family \(\{X_t : t \in T\}\) of random variables. It is discrete-time if \(T = \{0,1,2,\dots\}\) and continuous-time if \(T = [0,\infty)\). One realisation \(t \mapsto X_t(\omega)\) is a sample path. Markov chains are one kind of stochastic process.

2 · Martingales

  1. Martingale: \(\mathbb{E}|X_n| < \infty\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0,\dots,X_n] = X_n\), a fair game. Submartingale: \(\ge\). Supermartingale: \(\le\).
  2. Taking expectations: \(\mathbb{E}[X_n] = \mathbb{E}[X_0]\) for every fixed \(n\).
  3. Examples: a sum of independent mean-0 steps. A product of independent mean-1 factors: \(\mathbb{E}[Z_{n+1} \mid \text{past}] = Z_n\,\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}] = Z_n\).

3 · Optional stopping

  1. Stopping time \(T\): whether \(\{T = n\}\) happened can be decided from \(X_0,\dots,X_n\) alone. "The first time \(Z\) hits 10" is one; "the last time \(Z > 0\)" is not.
  2. Stopped process \(Z_{\min(n,T)}\) is again a martingale. Before \(T\) it moves fairly, and after \(T\) it is frozen.
  3. OST: \(\mathbb{E}[Z_T] = \mathbb{E}[Z_0]\) if \(T < \infty\) a.s. and at least one of these holds: \[\text{(a) } |Z_{\min(n,T)}| \le c \ \ \forall n; \qquad \text{(b) } T \le c; \qquad \text{(c) } \mathbb{E}[T] < \infty \ \text{and}\ \mathbb{E}\big[|Z_{n+1} - Z_n| \,\big|\, \text{past}\big] \le c.\]
  4. It can fail without them: the fair walk with \(T\) = the first hit of \(+10\) has \(\mathbb{E}[Z_T] = 10 \ne 0\). There, \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \infty\) and the walk is unbounded below.
  5. Finite \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) trick: if from every position there is probability \(\ge p\) of stopping within \(N\) steps, then \(T \le N \cdot \text{Geometric}(p)\), so \(\mathbb{E}[T] \le N/p\).

⚠ Course's condition (a) bounds \(Z_i\) itself; what is needed is a bound on the stopped process, plus \(T < \infty\). Always check a condition before applying OST.

Fair walk from 0 to +4 or −8

A walk starts at 0. Each step goes \(+1\) or \(-1\), with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. The walk stops as soon as it reaches \(+4\) or \(-8\).

Question: what is the probability that it stops at \(+4\)?

Key idea

A fair game stays fair when you stop it. So the average end point equals the start, 0.

The walk ends at \(+4\) or \(-8\). For the average to be 0, the two ends must balance like a seesaw around 0: \(+4\) is half as far away, so it must be hit twice as often. \(p = \tfrac23\).

Solution

Setup. \(X_n\) is a fair \(\pm1\) walk from 0. \(T\) = the first time it hits \(+4\) or \(-8\). \(p = P(X_T = +4)\).

  1. \(X_n\) is a martingale: \(\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid \text{past}] = X_n + \tfrac12(+1) + \tfrac12(-1) = X_n\).Why: each step has mean 0.
  2. \(T\) is finite, with \(\mathbb{E}[T] < \infty\).Why: from any point between \(-8\) and \(+4\), 12 up-steps in a row reach \(+4\); that has probability \(2^{-12}\). So each block of 12 steps ends the game with probability at least \(2^{-12}\), and \(T\) is at most 12 times a geometric variable (the finite-\(\mathbb{E}[T]\) trick).
  3. The stopped walk stays in \([-8, 4]\), so \(|X_{\min(n,T)}| \le 8\), and OST condition (a) gives \(\mathbb{E}[X_T] = X_0 = 0\).Why: it stops as soon as it reaches either end.
  4. \(X_T\) is \(+4\) or \(-8\), so \(4p - 8(1 - p) = 0\), i.e. \(12p = 8\) and \(p = \) \(\tfrac23\).Why: step 3.

Conclusion. The walk reaches \(+4\) first with probability \(\tfrac23\). ∎

Stop and bet on the next card being red

The 52 cards of a shuffled deck are turned face up one at a time. At any moment you like you may say 'stop'; you then win if the next card is red. If you never say stop, you are betting on the last card.

Question: is there a strategy that wins with probability more than \(\tfrac12\)? What is the best possible winning probability?

Key idea

If you stop now, you win with probability \(X = \dfrac{\text{reds left}}{\text{cards left}}\).

Flipping a card does not change \(X\) on average: a red makes it drop a little, a black makes it rise a little, and these cancel. So \(X\) is a fair game that starts at \(\tfrac12\).

Choosing when to stop cannot make a fair game favourable. Every strategy wins with probability exactly \(\tfrac12\).

Solution

Setup. 52 cards are turned over one by one. At any point you may stop and bet that the next card is red; if you never stop, you bet on the last card. After \(n\) cards have been shown, \(m = 52 - n\) remain, \(r\) of them red, and \(X_n = r/m\).

  1. \(X_n\) is a martingale.Why: the next card is red with probability \(\tfrac rm\), giving \(X_{n+1} = \tfrac{r-1}{m-1}\), and black with probability \(\tfrac{m-r}{m}\), giving \(\tfrac{r}{m-1}\). So \(\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid \text{past}] = \dfrac{r(r-1) + (m-r)r}{m(m-1)} = \dfrac{r(m-1)}{m(m-1)} = \dfrac rm\).
  2. A strategy is a stopping time \(T \le 51\) (you decide to stop using only the cards seen so far), and given what you have seen at time \(T\), \(P(\text{win}) = X_T\). So \(P(\text{win}) = \mathbb{E}[X_T]\).Why: the next card is a uniform pick from the remaining \(m\) cards, \(r\) of them red.
  3. \(T\) is bounded, so OST condition (b) gives \(\mathbb{E}[X_T] = X_0 = \tfrac{26}{52} = \tfrac12\).Why: \(T \le 51\).
  4. Every strategy wins with probability exactly \(\tfrac12\).Why: steps 2–3 hold for every stopping time.

Conclusion. No stopping rule beats \(\tfrac12\). ∎

2-D walk: expected exit time from the radius-10 disc

A walk starts at the origin of the integer grid. Each step moves one unit up, down, left or right, each with probability \(\tfrac14\).

Question: what is the expected number of steps until the walk first reaches a point at distance at least 10 from the origin (leaves the disc of radius 10)?

Key idea

Each step adds exactly 1 to \(x^2 + y^2\) on average (moving \(x\) to \(x \pm 1\) gives \(\tfrac12(x+1)^2 + \tfrac12(x-1)^2 = x^2 + 1\)).

So after \(T\) steps, the average of \(x^2 + y^2\) is the average of \(T\): \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[x_T^2 + y_T^2]\).

At the exit \(x^2 + y^2 \ge 100\), and a grid walk usually jumps past the circle. So \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) is a little more than 100.

Solution

Setup. A walk on the integer grid from the origin; each step moves one unit up, down, left or right with probability \(\tfrac14\) each. \(T\) = the first time \(X_n^2 + Y_n^2 \ge 100\) (the exit from the radius-10 disc).

  1. \(Z_n = X_n^2 + Y_n^2 - n\) is a martingale.Why: a step changes one coordinate by \(\pm1\). If it changes \(x\): \(\tfrac12(x+1)^2 + \tfrac12(x-1)^2 = x^2 + 1\). So \(\mathbb{E}[X^2 + Y^2 \text{ after} \mid \text{past}] = X^2 + Y^2 + 1\), and subtracting \(n + 1\) cancels the +1.
  2. OST condition (c) applies: \(\mathbb{E}[T] < \infty\), and before \(T\) the steps of \(Z\) are bounded.Why: before \(T\) the walk is at one of 305 interior points, from each of which 20 right-steps leave the disc (probability \(4^{-20}\)), so the finite-\(\mathbb{E}[T]\) trick applies. Inside the disc \(|x|, |y| \le 9\), so a step changes \(Z\) by at most about 20.
  3. \(\mathbb{E}[Z_T] = Z_0 = 0\), so \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[X_T^2 + Y_T^2]\).Why: OST, then rearrange.
  4. On exit \(X_T^2 + Y_T^2 \ge 100\), and it can overshoot: \((9,4) \to (10,4)\) jumps from \(r^2 = 97\) to \(116\) without landing on the circle.Why: the walk moves in unit steps, and the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 100\) has only 12 lattice points.
  5. Solving the exact linear system \(\text{rec}(v) = 1 + \text{average of rec over the 4 neighbours}\) on the 305 interior points gives \(\mathbb{E}[T] \approx\) \(104.69\). It is not 100.Why: first-step analysis; the overshoot of step 4 makes it larger than 100.

Conclusion. \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[R_T^2] \approx 104.69\). ∎

⚠ Course error. It defines \(T\) as the first time \(x^2 + y^2 = 100\) exactly and claims the walk stays inside the disc until then, which gives 100.
But a lattice walk can jump past the circle without landing on it (step 4). So that \(T\) is not the exit time, and its expectation is not 100.
The identity \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[R_T^2]\) is right; the value \(R_T^2 = 100\) is not.

4 · Wald's identity

If \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) are i.i.d. with \(\mathbb{E}|X| < \infty\), \(T\) is a stopping time with \(\mathbb{E}[T] < \infty\), and \(S_T = X_1 + \dots + X_T\), then \[\mathbb{E}[S_T] = \mathbb{E}[T]\,\mathbb{E}[X].\] (It is OST applied to \(S_n - n\,\mathbb{E}[X]\).)

Biased walk to +6

A walk starts at 0. Each step is \(+1\) with probability \(\tfrac23\) and \(-1\) with probability \(\tfrac13\), independently.

Question: what is the expected number of steps until it first reaches \(+6\)?

Key idea

Each step gains \(\tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac13\) on average. To gain 6, you need \(6 \div \tfrac13 = 18\) steps on average.

That is Wald's identity: average total = average number of steps × average step. The walk ends at exactly 6 (steps of size 1 cannot jump over it).

Solution

Setup. Steps \(X_i\) are \(+1\) with probability \(\tfrac23\) and \(-1\) with probability \(\tfrac13\), independently; \(S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n\). \(T\) = the first time \(S_n = 6\).

  1. \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac13\), and \(\mathbb{E}[T] < \infty\).Why: the walk drifts upward at rate \(\tfrac13\) per step, so it reaches any fixed level in finite expected time.
  2. \(S_T = 6\) exactly.Why: steps of size 1 cannot jump over 6.
  3. Wald's identity: \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = \mathbb{E}[T]\,\mathbb{E}[X]\), i.e. \(6 = \mathbb{E}[T]\cdot\tfrac13\), so \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \) \(18\).Why: \(T\) is a stopping time with finite mean and the steps are i.i.d.

Conclusion. It takes 18 steps on average to reach \(+6\). ∎

Roll until the first value repeats: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{sum}]\)

A fair die is rolled over and over until the value of the first roll comes up again.

Question: what is the expected sum of all the rolls, including the first and the last?

Key idea

Average sum = average number of rolls × average roll (Wald).

After the first roll, each roll matches it with probability \(\tfrac16\), so 6 more rolls on average: 7 rolls in total. Each averages 3.5. Sum: \(7 \times 3.5 = 24.5\).

Solution

Setup. Roll a die repeatedly; stop at the first roll that equals the first roll's value. \(T\) = the number of rolls, \(S_T\) = their sum.

  1. After the first roll, each later roll matches it with probability \(\tfrac16\), so \(T = 1 + \text{Geometric}(\tfrac16)\) and \(\mathbb{E}[T] = 1 + 6 = 7\).Why: a geometric wait with \(p = \tfrac16\) has mean 6.
  2. \(T\) is a stopping time for the i.i.d. rolls, with finite mean.Why: whether to stop is decided by the rolls seen so far.
  3. Wald: \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = 7\cdot3.5 = \) \(\tfrac{49}{2} = 24.5\).Why: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{roll}] = 3.5\).
  4. Check by conditioning on the first roll \(x\).
    On average 5 non-matching rolls follow, each uniform on the other 5 values (mean \(\tfrac{21 - x}{5}\)), then the matching \(x\).
    So \(\mathbb{E}[S \mid x] = x + 5\cdot\tfrac{21 - x}{5} + x = 21 + x\), and averaging over \(x\) gives \(21 + 3.5 = 24.5\) ✓.Why: the number of misses before a match is geometric with mean 5.

Conclusion. The expected sum is 24.5. ∎

Fair walk, boundaries \(-4\) and \(+8\)

A walk starts at 0 with steps \(\pm1\), each with probability \(\tfrac12\). It stops as soon as it reaches \(-4\) or \(+8\).

Question: (a) what is the probability that it stops at \(+8\)? (b) What is the expected number of steps until it stops?

Key idea

(a) Seesaw. The average end point is the start, 0. \(+8\) is twice as far as \(-4\), so it is hit half as often: \(8p = 4(1-p)\), \(p = \tfrac13\).

(b) Squares. \(S^2\) grows by exactly 1 per step on average, so \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[S_T^2] = 64\cdot\tfrac13 + 16\cdot\tfrac23 = 32\). In general: boundaries \(-a, +b\) give \(p = \tfrac{a}{a+b}\) and \(\mathbb{E}[T] = ab\).

Solution

Setup. A fair \(\pm1\) walk \(S_n\) from 0, stopped at \(T\) = the first hit of \(-4\) or \(+8\). \(p = P(\text{hit } +8 \text{ first})\).

  1. (a) \(S_n\) is a martingale and the stopped walk stays in \([-4, 8]\), so \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = 0\): \(8p - 4(1 - p) = 0\), i.e. \(12p = 4\) and \(p = \) \(\tfrac13\).Why: OST condition (a), as in the first problem of this section.
  2. (b) \(S_n^2 - n\) is a martingale.Why: \(\mathbb{E}[(S + \varepsilon)^2 - (n+1) \mid S] = S^2 + 2S\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] - n - 1 = S^2 + 0 + 1 - n - 1 = S^2 - n\).
  3. \(\mathbb{E}[T] < \infty\) and the increments are bounded before \(T\), so OST (c) gives \(\mathbb{E}[S_T^2 - T] = 0\), i.e. \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[S_T^2]\).Why: the finite-\(\mathbb{E}[T]\) trick; \(|S| \le 8\) before \(T\).
  4. \(\mathbb{E}[S_T^2] = 64\cdot\tfrac13 + 16\cdot\tfrac23 = \tfrac{64 + 32}{3} = \) \(32\). (In general, \(ab\) for boundaries \(-a\) and \(+b\).)Why: \(S_T = 8\) with probability \(\tfrac13\) and \(-4\) with \(\tfrac23\) (part a).

Conclusion. \(\tfrac13\) and 32. ∎

Biased walk (\(p = \tfrac23\)), boundaries \(-4\) and \(+8\)

A walk starts at 0. Each step is \(+1\) with probability \(p = \tfrac23\) and \(-1\) with probability \(q = \tfrac13\). It stops as soon as it reaches \(-4\) or \(+8\).

Question: (a) what is the probability that it stops at \(+8\)? (b) What is the expected number of steps until it stops?

Key idea

(a) \(S\) is no longer fair (it drifts up), so the seesaw on \(S\) fails. Find a quantity that is fair: \(M = (\tfrac12)^S\). An up-step halves it and a down-step doubles it: \(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac13\cdot2 = 1\), so on average it doesn't move. Balance \(M\) instead of \(S\).

(b) \(S\) gains \(\tfrac13\) per step on average, so \(\mathbb{E}[T] = 3\,\mathbb{E}[S_T]\).

Solution

Setup. Steps are \(+1\) with probability \(p = \tfrac23\) and \(-1\) with \(q = \tfrac13\). \(S_n\) starts at 0 and stops at \(T\) = the first hit of \(-4\) or \(+8\). \(r = P(\text{hit } +8 \text{ first})\).

  1. \(M_n = (q/p)^{S_n} = (\tfrac12)^{S_n}\) is a martingale.Why: \(\mathbb{E}[(\tfrac12)^{\varepsilon}] = \tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac13\cdot2 = \tfrac13 + \tfrac23 = 1\), so each step multiplies \(M\) by a factor with mean 1.
  2. The stopped \(M\) is bounded (between \(2^{-8}\) and \(2^4\)), so OST (a) gives \(\mathbb{E}[M_T] = M_0 = 1\): \(r\cdot2^{-8} + (1 - r)\cdot2^{4} = 1\).Why: \(S_T = 8\) gives \(M = 2^{-8}\); \(S_T = -4\) gives \(M = 2^4 = 16\).
  3. \(\tfrac{r}{256} + 16 - 16r = 1\), so \(r\big(16 - \tfrac{1}{256}\big) = 15\) and \(r = \dfrac{15\cdot256}{4095} = \) \(\tfrac{256}{273} \approx 0.93773\).Why: \(16 - \tfrac1{256} = \tfrac{4095}{256}\), and \(\tfrac{3840}{4095} = \tfrac{256}{273}\).
  4. (b) \(S_n - \tfrac n3\) is a martingale, so \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = \tfrac13\mathbb{E}[T]\), i.e. \(\mathbb{E}[T] = 3\,\mathbb{E}[S_T]\).Why: each step has mean \(\tfrac13\); OST (c).
  5. \(\mathbb{E}[S_T] = 8r - 4(1 - r) = 12r - 4 = \tfrac{3072 - 1092}{273} = \tfrac{1980}{273} = \tfrac{660}{91}\), so \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \tfrac{1980}{91} \approx\) \(21.75824\).Why: substitute \(r = \tfrac{256}{273}\).

Conclusion. \(\tfrac{256}{273}\) and \(\tfrac{1980}{91}\). ∎

\(\mathbb{E}[\text{tosses until HHTTHTT}]\)

A fair coin is tossed until the seven tosses H, H, T, T, H, T, T appear in a row.

Question: what is the expected number of tosses?

Key idea

Before each toss a new gambler arrives with $1 and bets on the pattern, doubling his money on every correct letter. Every bet is fair, so the casino's average profit is 0.

The casino takes in $1 per toss, so $\(T\) in total. It pays out whatever the gamblers hold at the end. So \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) = the money held at the end.

At the end, a gambler still has money only if his letters so far are both the start and the end of the pattern. For HHTTHTT only the full pattern qualifies: \(2^7 = 128\).

Solution

Setup. Toss a fair coin until HHTTHTT appears; \(T\) = the number of tosses.
Before each toss a new gambler arrives with $1 and bets it on that toss being H (the pattern's first letter).
If he wins, his money doubles and he bets it all on the next toss being the pattern's next letter, and so on. He stops when he loses (keeping nothing) or completes the pattern.
\(W_n\) = the total money held by all gamblers after toss \(n\).

  1. \(M_n = W_n - n\) is a martingale.Why: every bet is double-or-nothing on a fair coin, so it has expected gain 0; exactly $1 of new money enters before each toss.
  2. OST applies, so \(\mathbb{E}[W_T - T] = 0\), i.e. \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[W_T]\).Why: \(\mathbb{E}[T] < \infty\) (each block of 7 tosses completes the pattern with probability \(2^{-7}\)), and before \(T\) at most 7 gamblers are active, each holding at most \(2^7\), so the increments are bounded.
  3. At time \(T\), a gambler who started \(L\) tosses ago still has money exactly when the last \(L\) tosses equal the first \(L\) letters of the pattern.
    Those last \(L\) tosses are also the last \(L\) letters of the pattern (it was just completed).
    So he is alive iff the length-\(L\) prefix equals the length-\(L\) suffix, and then he holds \(2^L\).Why: he has won all \(L\) of his bets.
  4. For HHTTHTT no proper prefix equals the suffix of the same length: H/T, HH/TT, HHT/HTT, HHTT/THTT, HHTTH/TTHTT, HHTTHT/HTTHTT. Only \(L = 7\) works, so \(W_T = 2^7 = 128\).Why: compare each pair (table below).
  5. \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \) \(128\). (Contrast HH: the prefix H equals the suffix H, so \(W_T = 2 + 4 = 6\).)Why: step 2.

Conclusion. HHTTHTT takes 128 tosses on average. ∎
