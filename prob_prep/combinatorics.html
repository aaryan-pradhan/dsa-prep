1 · Multiplication & addition

  1. Product rule. If step 1 has \(a\) ways and, for each of them, step 2 has \(b\) ways, then doing both has \(a\cdot b\) ways. For \(n\) steps: \(a_1a_2\cdots a_n\).
  2. Sum rule. If \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint (you do \(A\) or \(B\), never both), there are \(a + b\) ways.

Books and boxes

There are 3 different maths books and 4 different physics books.

Question: (a) In how many ways can you pick one maths book and one physics book? (b) In how many ways can you pick one book from all 7? (c) Separately: in how many ways can 5 different objects be put into 3 different boxes, if a box may be left empty?

Key idea

"And" in sequence: multiply. "Or", disjoint: add.

Picture

"And" in sequence → multiply. "Or" between separate options → add.

(a) one maths book and then one physics book: 3 × 4. (b) one book from either pile: 3 + 4. (c) each of the 5 objects independently picks a box: 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3.

Solution

Setup. There are 3 different maths books and 4 different physics books. In (c), 5 different objects go into 3 different boxes, and a box may stay empty.

  1. (a) One maths book and one physics book: \(3 \cdot 4 = \) \(12\).Why: first pick the maths book (3 ways). Whichever one you picked, there are then 4 ways to pick the physics book. Every (maths, physics) pair comes from exactly one such pair of choices, so the pairs fill a \(3 \times 4\) table: product rule.
  2. (b) One book from all 7: \(3 + 4 = \) \(7\).Why: the chosen book is either a maths book (3 ways) or a physics book (4 ways). No book is both, so the two cases don't overlap and their counts add: sum rule.
  3. (c) Place the objects one at a time. Object 1 has 3 choices of box, object 2 has 3, …, object 5 has 3. Total \(3^5 = \) \(243\).Why: empty boxes are allowed, so no earlier choice restricts a later one. A placement is exactly a list of 5 box choices, so the product rule gives \(3\cdot3\cdot3\cdot3\cdot3\).

Conclusion. 12, 7 and 243. ∎

2 · Linear permutations

  1. \(n! = n(n-1)\cdots1\), and \(0! = 1\).
  2. Ordered choice of \(r\) from \(n\), no repeats: \(\displaystyle P(n,r) = n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\). (product rule)
  3. With repeats allowed: \(n^r\).
  4. Arranging \(n\) objects with \(p_1,\dots,p_k\) identical copies: \(\displaystyle\frac{n!}{p_1!\cdots p_k!}\). (each word is counted \(p_1!\cdots p_k!\) times among the \(n!\) labelled orders)

Quick counts

Question:
(a) In how many ways can 3 of 5 people stand in a line (a different order counts as a different line)?
(b) How many 3-letter words can be made from the letters A, B, C if letters may repeat (like AAB)?
(c) How many 4-digit PINs are there (digits 0–9, repeats and leading zeros allowed)?
(d) In how many different orders can the 7 letters of BALLOON be arranged?

Key idea

Ask two things: does order matter, and are repeats allowed? Identical items: divide out their internal orders.

Picture

Ask two questions: does order matter, and can things repeat? Ordered, no repeats: 5 × 4 × 3. Ordered with repeats: 3 × 3 × 3 and 10⁴.

Identical letters: pretend they are different (7! orders), then notice each real word was counted once per way of shuffling the identical copies among themselves: 2! for the L's, 2! for the O's.

Solution

  1. (a) Line up 3 of 5 people: \(5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = \) \(60\).Why: position 1 can be any of the 5 people; position 2 any of the 4 not already placed; position 3 any of the remaining 3. Order matters (a different order is a different line), so we multiply.
  2. (b) 3-letter words from \(\{A, B, C\}\), repeats allowed: \(3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = \) \(27\).Why: each of the 3 positions independently gets one of 3 letters.
  3. (c) 4-digit PINs: \(10^4 = \) \(10000\).Why: each of the 4 positions independently gets one of 10 digits (leading zeros are fine for a PIN).
  4. (d) BALLOON has 7 letters: B, A, L, L, O, O, N. First pretend the copies are different: \(L_1, L_2, O_1, O_2\). Then there are \(7! = 5040\) orders of 7 distinct symbols.Why: 7 choices for the first place, 6 for the second, …, 1 for the last.
  5. Each real word appears exactly \(2! \cdot 2! = 4\) times among those 5040 labelled orders.Why: from one word, the labels \(L_1, L_2\) can be put on the two L's in 2 ways, and independently the labels \(O_1, O_2\) on the two O's in 2 ways.
  6. So BALLOON has \(\dfrac{5040}{4} = \) \(1260\) rearrangements.Why: divide the labelled count by how many times each word is repeated (step 5).

Conclusion. 60, 27, 10000 and 1260. ∎

3 · Circular permutations

  1. Arrangements that differ only by a rotation count as the same.
  2. \(n\) distinct objects in a circle: \(n!/n = (n-1)!\). No rotation other than the identity fixes an arrangement of distinct objects, so each circle appears exactly \(n\) times among the \(n!\) lines.
  3. If flips also count as the same (necklaces), and \(n \ge 3\): \(\tfrac{(n-1)!}{2}\). For \(n \ge 3\), a circle and its mirror image are different circles.

Table and necklace

(a) 6 people sit around a round table. Two seatings count as the same if one is a rotation of the other (everyone moves the same number of seats round).
(b) 6 different beads are threaded on a necklace. Two necklaces count as the same if one is a rotation of the other or a flip (turning the necklace over).

Question: how many different seatings are there in (a), and how many different necklaces in (b)?

Key idea

Fix one person's seat to kill the rotations. Halve again to kill the flips.

Picture

Count lines, then divide by how many lines give the same circle. 6 people in a line: 720. Each round table is 6 of those lines (its rotations): 720 ÷ 6 = 120.

A necklace can also be turned over, which pairs up the circles: 120 ÷ 2 = 60.

Solution

Setup. (a) 6 people at a round table; two seatings are the same if one is a rotation of the other. (b) 6 different beads on a necklace; two necklaces are the same if one is a rotation or a flip of the other.

  1. Seat the 6 people on 6 numbered chairs: \(6! = 720\) seatings.Why: 6 choices for chair 1, 5 for chair 2, and so on.
  2. Each round-table arrangement corresponds to exactly 6 of these numbered seatings: its 6 rotations.Why: rotating by 1, 2, …, 5 places moves every person to a different chair, and the people are all different, so the 6 rotations are 6 different numbered seatings.
  3. (a) So there are \(720 / 6 = \) \(120\) round-table arrangements. (Equivalently, fix person 1's chair and arrange the other 5: \(5! = 120\).)Why: 720 numbered seatings, grouped 6 per arrangement (step 2).
  4. A circle and its mirror image are different circles when there are at least 3 beads.Why: in a circle, bead 1 has a clockwise neighbour \(u\) and an anticlockwise neighbour \(v\), and \(u \ne v\) when there are at least 3 beads. A rotation keeps \(u\) as the clockwise neighbour; the mirror image makes \(v\) the clockwise neighbour. So no rotation equals the mirror image.
  5. (b) Each necklace is exactly 2 circles (a circle and its mirror image), so there are \(120 / 2 = \) \(60\) necklaces.Why: step 4, applied to the 120 circles of step 3.

Conclusion. 120 seatings and 60 necklaces. ∎

⚠ Course omits \(n \ge 3\) for the \(\div 2\). For \(n \le 2\) a necklace equals its own flip, which is why step 4 needs at least 3 beads.

4 · Combinations

  1. Unordered choice of \(r\) from \(n\): \(\displaystyle\binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\). Each \(r\)-set appears \(r!\) times among the \(P(n,r)\) ordered choices.
  2. \(\binom{n}{r} = \binom{n}{n-r}\). (choosing what to keep = choosing what to drop)
  3. \(\binom{n}{r}\) is largest at \(r = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\) or \(\lceil n/2 \rceil\). (\(\binom{n}{r+1}\big/\binom{n}{r} = \frac{n-r}{r+1} \ge 1 \iff r \le \frac{n-1}{2}\))

Committees and subsets

Question: (a) How many different teams of 2 can be chosen from 3 people? (b) How many 4-element subsets does a 10-element set have?

Key idea

Order does not matter, so use \(\binom{n}{r}\).

Picture

Order doesn't matter, so count ordered picks and divide by the orders of each group. 3 × 2 ordered pairs, each team appearing twice: 3 teams. In general C(n, r) = P(n, r) ÷ r!.

Solution

  1. (a) Teams of 2 from 3 people: ordered picks give \(3 \cdot 2 = 6\), and each team appears twice (in its 2 orders), so there are \(6 / 2 = \) \(3\) teams. This is \(\binom32\).Why: a team is an unordered set, so the orders \((x, y)\) and \((y, x)\) give the same team.
  2. (b) 4-element subsets of a 10-element set: \(\binom{10}{4} = \dfrac{10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7}{4!} = \dfrac{5040}{24} = \) \(210\).Why: \(10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7\) ordered picks, and each subset appears in \(4! = 24\) orders.

Conclusion. 3 and 210. ∎

Languages

There are 69 people. Each person speaks some languages, and everyone speaks the same number of languages. For any two people A and B: A speaks at least one language that B doesn't, and B speaks at least one language that A doesn't.

Question: what is the smallest possible total number of different languages?

Key idea

Equal-size sets automatically satisfy the condition as soon as they are distinct. So you need \(\binom{n}{r} \ge 69\) for some \(r\).

Picture

Equal-sized sets that are all different automatically satisfy the condition: neither can contain the other.

So the question is: how few languages give at least 69 different sets of one size? The most sets of one size come from choosing half the languages. That first reaches 69 at 8 languages (70 sets of 4).

Solution

Setup. 69 people. Person \(p\) speaks a set \(L_p\) of languages, all sets have the same size \(r\), and \(n\) languages exist in total. Condition: for any two people \(a, b\), \(a\) speaks a language \(b\) doesn't, and \(b\) speaks one \(a\) doesn't. We want the smallest \(n\).

Claim. The minimum is 8.

  1. The condition says: neither of \(L_a, L_b\) contains the other.Why: \(a\) speaks a language \(b\) doesn't exactly when \(L_a \not\subseteq L_b\); the second half gives \(L_b \not\subseteq L_a\).
  2. In particular, the 69 sets are all different.Why: if \(L_a = L_b\), then \(L_a \subseteq L_b\), which step 1 forbids.
  3. With \(n\) languages and all sets of size \(r\), there are at most \(\binom{n}{r}\) different sets. So we need \(\binom{n}{r} \ge 69\).Why: step 2: 69 different sets of size \(r\).
  4. For each \(n\), the largest \(\binom{n}{r}\) is at \(r = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\). The row maxima for \(n = 1, \dots, 7\) are \(1, 2, 3, 6, 10, 20, 35\), all less than 69. So \(n \ge 8\).Why: §4.3: \(\binom{n}{r}\) is largest in the middle of the row. The row maxima increase with \(n\), and \(\binom73 = 35 < 69\).
  5. \(n = 8\) works: \(\binom84 = 70 \ge 69\), so give the 69 people 69 different 4-element sets of languages.Why: there are enough 4-sets.
  6. These sets satisfy the condition.Why: if \(L_a \subseteq L_b\) and both have 4 elements, then \(L_a = L_b\). They are different, so neither contains the other, which is the condition (step 1).

Conclusion. At least 8 languages are needed (step 4), and 8 are enough (steps 5–6). ∎

(Without the equal-size assumption the answer is still 8, by Sperner's theorem.)

5 · Identities (with counting proofs)

  1. Pascal: \(\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}\). Split on whether element \(n\) is chosen.
  2. Hockey stick: \(\displaystyle\sum_{j=r}^{n} \binom{j}{r} = \binom{n+1}{r+1}\). Choose \(r+1\) numbers from \(\{1,\dots,n+1\}\) and split on the largest one, \(j+1\). The other \(r\) come from \(\{1,\dots,j\}\).
  3. Vandermonde: \(\displaystyle\sum_k \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{r-k} = \binom{m+n}{r}\). Choose \(r\) from \(m\) men and \(n\) women, splitting on \(k\) = the number of men.

Sum of squares

\(\binom{n}{k}\) (“\(n\) choose \(k\)”) is the number of ways to choose \(k\) things from \(n\).

Question: find the value of \(\binom{10}{0}^2 + \binom{10}{1}^2 + \dots + \binom{10}{10}^2\).

Key idea

Write \(\binom{10}{k}^2\) as \(\binom{10}{k}\binom{10}{10-k}\), which is Vandermonde.

Picture

Count one thing two ways. Pick 10 people from 10 men and 10 women: C(20, 10) directly.

Or split by k = the number of men: C(10, k) ways for the men times C(10, 10 − k) = C(10, k) for the women. Adding over k gives the sum of squares.

Solution

Claim. \(\binom{10}{0}^2 + \binom{10}{1}^2 + \dots + \binom{10}{10}^2 = \binom{20}{10} = \) \(184756\).

  1. \(\binom{10}{k} = \binom{10}{10-k}\), so each term \(\binom{10}{k}^2 = \binom{10}{k}\binom{10}{10-k}\).Why: choosing \(k\) items to keep is the same as choosing the \(10 - k\) items to leave out (§4.2).
  2. Count the 10-person teams from a group of 10 men and 10 women in two ways. Directly: \(\binom{20}{10}\).Why: choose 10 of 20 people.
  3. By the number \(k\) of men: \(\binom{10}{k}\) ways to pick the men and \(\binom{10}{10-k}\) to pick the women. Summing over \(k = 0, \dots, 10\) gives \(\sum_k \binom{10}{k}\binom{10}{10-k}\).Why: every team has some number \(k\) of men, and different \(k\) are different teams (sum rule); for a fixed \(k\), men and women are chosen independently (product rule).
  4. So the sum equals \(\binom{20}{10} = 184756\).Why: steps 1–3 count the same teams.

Conclusion. The sum of the squares is \(\binom{20}{10} = 184756\). ∎

Red and green objects

There are 100 different red objects and 100 different green objects. You choose some red objects (possibly none) and some green objects so that the number of green ones is exactly one more than the number of red ones.

Question: in how many ways can you do this? Write the answer as one binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{r}\), and report \(n + r\).

Key idea

\(\sum_i \binom{100}{i}\binom{100}{i+1}\). Flip the second factor to \(\binom{100}{99-i}\) and apply Vandermonde.

Picture

Turn "i + 1 greens chosen" into "99 − i greens left out". Then choosing i reds and leaving out 99 − i greens is the same as picking a 99-element set from all 200 objects, split by how many reds it contains.

Solution

Setup. 100 different red objects and 100 different green ones. We choose \(i\) reds and \(i + 1\) greens, for any \(i\) from 0 to 99.

Claim. \(\binom{200}{99}\), so \(n + r = 299\).

  1. For a fixed \(i\): \(\binom{100}{i}\binom{100}{i+1}\) ways.Why: choose the reds and the greens independently (product rule).
  2. Total: \(\sum_{i=0}^{99} \binom{100}{i}\binom{100}{i+1}\).Why: different \(i\) are different selections (sum rule).
  3. \(\binom{100}{i+1} = \binom{100}{99-i}\).Why: choosing \(i + 1\) greens to take is the same as choosing the \(100 - (i+1) = 99 - i\) greens to leave out.
  4. So the total is \(\sum_i \binom{100}{i}\binom{100}{99-i}\). This counts the 99-element subsets of all 200 objects, split by \(i\) = the number of reds in the subset. So it equals \(\binom{200}{99}\).Why: a 99-subset with \(i\) reds has \(99 - i\) greens (Vandermonde). Concretely: mark the chosen reds and the left-out greens. That is \(i + (99 - i) = 99\) marked objects, and every 99-subset arises from exactly one choice.

Conclusion. The number of choices is \(\binom{200}{99}\). ∎

⚠ The original course statement says “one or more” reds, but the course's sum includes \(i = 0\). With at least 1 red, subtract the \(i = 0\) term \(\binom{100}{0}\binom{100}{1} = 100\): the answer is \(\binom{200}{99} - 100\).

6 · Binomial theorem

  1. \(\displaystyle(x+y)^n = \sum_k \binom{n}{k} x^k y^{n-k}\). A term \(x^ky^{n-k}\) comes from choosing which \(k\) of the \(n\) factors contribute \(x\).
  2. \(\sum_k \binom{n}{k} = 2^n\). (\(x=y=1\); or: each element is in or out)
  3. For \(n \ge 1\): \(\sum_{k \text{ even}} = \sum_{k \text{ odd}} = 2^{n-1}\). (\(x=1, y=-1\) gives \(0 = \text{even} - \text{odd}\))
  4. \(\sum_k k\binom{n}{k} = n\,2^{n-1}\). (a committee with a chair: pick the chair (\(n\)), then any subset of the rest (\(2^{n-1}\)))
  5. \(\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{n}{k} = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k+1}\). (expand both sides as factorials)

Coefficient

Question: when \((x + y)^8\) is multiplied out, what number multiplies \(x^2y^6\)?

Key idea

Choose which 2 of the 8 factors contribute \(x\).

Picture

Multiplying out = choosing x or y from each bracket. You get x²y⁶ exactly when 2 of the 8 brackets give x: C(8, 2) = 28 ways.

Solution

  1. \((x+y)^8\) is a product of 8 factors \((x + y)\). Expanding means choosing \(x\) or \(y\) from each factor and multiplying the choices.Why: that is what multiplying out brackets does.
  2. A choice gives \(x^2y^6\) exactly when \(x\) is taken from 2 of the factors and \(y\) from the other 6. There are \(\binom82 = 28\) ways to pick those 2 factors.Why: choose which 2 of the 8 factors contribute \(x\).
  3. Each such choice contributes 1 to the coefficient, so the coefficient is \(28\).Why: all the terms \(x^2y^6\) are added together.

Conclusion. The coefficient of \(x^2y^6\) is 28. ∎

Odd binomials

Question: find \(\binom{18}{1} + \binom{18}{3} + \binom{18}{5} + \dots + \binom{18}{17}\), the sum of the entries of row 18 of Pascal's triangle with odd bottom index.

Key idea

Odd sum = even sum = half of \(2^{18}\).

Picture

Plug in numbers. x = y = 1 gives all entries summing to 2¹⁸. x = 1, y = −1 gives (even entries) − (odd entries) = 0. So each half is 2¹⁷.

Solution

Setup. Let \(E = \sum_{k \text{ even}} \binom{18}{k}\) and \(O = \sum_{k \text{ odd}} \binom{18}{k}\). We want \(O\).

  1. \(E + O = 2^{18}\).Why: put \(x = y = 1\) in \((x + y)^{18} = \sum_k \binom{18}{k}x^ky^{18-k}\): the right side becomes the sum of all the coefficients.
  2. \(E - O = 0\).Why: put \(x = -1, y = 1\): the left side is \(0^{18} = 0\), and on the right each term is \((-1)^k\binom{18}{k}\), which is \(+\) for even \(k\) and \(-\) for odd \(k\).
  3. Adding and subtracting: \(E = O = \tfrac{2^{18}}{2} = 2^{17} = \) \(131072\).Why: from step 2, \(E = O\); substituting into step 1 gives \(2O = 2^{18}\).

Conclusion. The odd-index sum is \(2^{17} = 131072\). ∎

Weighted sum

Take the entries of row 18 of Pascal's triangle with even bottom index \(k = 0, 2, 4, \dots, 18\), and divide each one by \(k + 1\).

Question: find \(\dfrac{1}{1}\binom{18}{0} + \dfrac13\binom{18}{2} + \dfrac15\binom{18}{4} + \dots + \dfrac{1}{19}\binom{18}{18}\).

Key idea

\(\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{18}{k} = \frac{1}{19}\binom{19}{k+1}\). The even \(k\) become the odd indices of 19.

Picture

Absorb the fraction into a bigger binomial: C(18, k)/(k + 1) = C(19, k + 1)/19.

Then even k in row 18 become odd positions in row 19, which add to half of 2¹⁹. Divide by 19.

Solution

Setup. The sum is \(\sum_{k = 0, 2, 4, \dots, 18} \dfrac{1}{k+1}\binom{18}{k}\).

  1. \(\dfrac{1}{k+1}\binom{18}{k} = \dfrac{1}{19}\binom{19}{k+1}\).Why: \(\dfrac{1}{k+1}\cdot\dfrac{18!}{k!\,(18-k)!} = \dfrac{18!}{(k+1)!\,(18-k)!}\), and \(\dfrac{1}{19}\cdot\dfrac{19!}{(k+1)!\,(18-k)!} = \dfrac{18!}{(k+1)!\,(18-k)!}\). Both sides are equal.
  2. So the sum is \(\dfrac{1}{19}\sum_{k \text{ even}} \binom{19}{k+1}\). As \(k\) runs over \(0, 2, \dots, 18\), \(k + 1\) runs over \(1, 3, \dots, 19\): all the odd indices of row 19.Why: step 1 applied to every term.
  3. The odd-index sum of row 19 is \(2^{19} / 2 = 2^{18}\).Why: same argument as the previous problem: even and odd sums are equal and add up to \(2^{19}\).
  4. The answer is \(\dfrac{2^{18}}{19} = \dfrac{262144}{19} \approx 13797.05\).Why: steps 2 and 3.

Conclusion. The sum equals \(\tfrac{262144}{19}\). ∎

\((1+\sqrt2)^{3000}\)

Question: when \((1+\sqrt2)^{3000}\) is written as a decimal, what is the 100th digit after the decimal point?

Key idea

\((1+\sqrt2)^n + (1-\sqrt2)^n\) is an integer, and \(|1-\sqrt2|^{3000}\) is tiny and positive.

Picture

Pair (1 + √2)ⁿ with its twin (1 − √2)ⁿ. Their sum is always a whole number (the √2 terms cancel).

(1 − √2)³⁰⁰⁰ is positive (even power) and incredibly small. So (1 + √2)³⁰⁰⁰ = whole number − tiny: it sits just below an integer, and its decimals are 0.99999…

Solution

Setup. Let \(\alpha = 1 + \sqrt2\) and \(\beta = 1 - \sqrt2\).

Claim. The 100th digit after the decimal point of \(\alpha^{3000}\) is \(9\).

  1. \(N = \alpha^{3000} + \beta^{3000}\) is an integer.Why: by the binomial theorem, \(\alpha^{n} = \sum_k \binom{n}{k}(\sqrt2)^k\) and \(\beta^n = \sum_k \binom{n}{k}(-\sqrt2)^k\). Adding them, the terms with odd \(k\) cancel (since \((-\sqrt2)^k = -(\sqrt2)^k\)), and the terms with even \(k\) double to \(2\binom{n}{k}2^{k/2}\), which is an integer.
  2. \(\varepsilon = \beta^{3000} = (\sqrt2 - 1)^{3000}\) is positive.Why: 3000 is even, so \((1 - \sqrt2)^{3000} = (\sqrt2 - 1)^{3000}\), and \(\sqrt2 - 1 > 0\).
  3. \(\varepsilon < 10^{-1100}\).Why: \(\sqrt2 < 1.42\), so \(\sqrt2 - 1 < 0.42\). And \(\log_{10} 0.42 \approx -0.377\), so \(0.42^{3000} \approx 10^{-1131} < 10^{-1100}\).
  4. \(\alpha^{3000} = N - \varepsilon = (N - 1) + (1 - \varepsilon)\).Why: rearrange step 1: \(\alpha^{3000} = N - \beta^{3000}\).
  5. \(N - 1\) is an integer and \(0 < 1 - \varepsilon < 1\), so the decimal part of \(\alpha^{3000}\) is \(1 - \varepsilon = 0.999\ldots\) with at least 1100 nines after the point.Why: \(1 - \varepsilon > 1 - 10^{-1100} = 0.\underbrace{99\ldots9}_{1100}\) (steps 2–3).
  6. In particular the 100th digit is 9.Why: \(100 \le 1100\).

Conclusion. \((1+\sqrt2)^{3000}\) is a tiny bit less than an integer, so its first 1100 decimals are all 9. ∎

⚠ Course doesn't note that \(\varepsilon > 0\) (step 2: that is what makes the decimals \(0.999\ldots\), not \(0.000\ldots\)) and doesn't bound \(\varepsilon\) (step 3).

7 · Complement, block, gap

  1. Complement: #wanted = #all − #unwanted. Use it for "at least one".
  2. Block: glue items that must be adjacent into one block, then multiply by the orders inside the block.
  3. Gap: to keep items apart, arrange the others first and drop the separated items into distinct gaps.

Four quick ones

A 'word' here means any string of letters A–Z.

Question:
(a) How many 4-letter words contain at least one A?
(b) 10 people, including A and B, stand in a line. In how many of the orders are A and B next to each other?
(c) In how many are A and B not next to each other?
(d) 10 points lie in a row. In how many ways can you choose 4 of them so that no two chosen points are next to each other?

Key idea

(a) complement, (b) block, (c) gaps between 8 people, (d) the 4 chosen points go into gaps between the 6 unchosen ones.

Picture

(a) Complement: all words minus words with no A. (b) Block: glue A and B together, arrange 9 units, then 2 orders inside. (c) Gaps: seat the other 8, then drop A and B into 2 of the 9 gaps. (d) Gaps again: the 6 unchosen points leave 7 gaps; put each chosen point in its own gap.

Solution

  1. (a) All 4-letter words: \(26^4 = 456976\). Words with no A: \(25^4 = 390625\). At least one A: \(456976 - 390625 = \) \(66351\).Why: complement: every word either has an A or has none, never both.
  2. (b) A and B adjacent: glue A and B into one block. Then there are 9 units (the block and 8 other people): \(9! = 362880\) orders. Inside the block, AB or BA: 2 orders. Total \(362880 \cdot 2 = \) \(725760\).Why: every line with A next to B comes from exactly one order of the 9 units together with one order inside the block.
  3. (c) A and B not adjacent.
    Line up the other 8 people: \(8! = 40320\) ways. That creates 9 gaps (before the first, between neighbours, after the last).
    Put A and B into 2 different gaps: \(\binom92 = 36\) choices of gaps, times 2 for which of them gets A.
    Total \(40320 \cdot 36 \cdot 2 = \) \(2903040\).Why: A and B are non-adjacent exactly when someone stands between them, i.e. when they are in different gaps. Check: \(10! - 725760 = 3628800 - 725760 = 2903040\).

  2. So \(x_n = A\cdot2^n + B\cdot3^n\) for constants \(A, B\).Why: a sum of two solutions is a solution, and a sequence is fixed by \(x_1, x_2\); since \(A, B\) can be chosen to match any \(x_1, x_2\) (step 3 solves for them), this form covers every solution.
  3. \(n = 1\): \(2A + 3B = 7\). \(n = 2\): \(4A + 9B = 22\). Doubling the first gives \(4A + 6B = 14\); subtracting from the second gives \(3B = 8\), so \(B = \tfrac83\). Then \(2A = 7 - 8 = -1\), so \(A = -\tfrac12\).Why: elimination.
  4. So \(x_n = -2^{n-1} + 8\cdot3^{n-1}\). Check: \(x_3 = -4 + 72 = 68\) and \(5\cdot22 - 6\cdot7 = 110 - 42 = 68\) ✓.Why: \(-\tfrac12 2^n = -2^{n-1}\) and \(\tfrac83 3^n = 8\cdot3^{n-1}\).
  5. (b) \(y^2 - 4y + 4 = (y - 2)^2\): a double root 2, so \(x_n = (A + Bn)2^n\).Why: for a double root \(r\), both \(r^n\) and \(nr^n\) solve the recurrence (§12.2).
  6. \(n = 1\): \(2(A + B) = 12\), so \(A + B = 6\). \(n = 2\): \(4(A + 2B) = 24\), so \(A + 2B = 6\). Subtracting: \(B = 0\), \(A = 6\).Why: elimination.
  7. So \(x_n = 6\cdot2^n\). Check: \(x_3 = 48\) and \(4\cdot24 - 4\cdot12 = 96 - 48 = 48\) ✓.Why: substitute.

Conclusion. (a) \(x_n = -2^{n-1} + 8\cdot3^{n-1}\); (b) \(x_n = 6\cdot2^n\). ∎

⚠ Course gets (a) \(x_n = 2^n + 2\cdot3^n\), which gives \(x_1 = 8 \ne 7\), and (b) \(A = B = 2\), which gives \(x_1 = 8 \ne 12\).

13 · Bijection

To count \(A\), build a map to an easy set \(B\) and show it is one-to-one and onto (usually by giving the inverse map). Checking only one direction is the classic mistake.

Increasing words & lattice paths

Question: (a) How many 4-letter words (letters A–Z) have strictly increasing letters, like ADGX? (b) How many routes go from \((0,0)\) to \((4,3)\) on the grid, if each step is one unit right or one unit up?

Key idea

(a) word ↔ its set of letters. (b) path ↔ a word of 4 R's and 3 U's.

Picture

Match each object with something easy to count. An increasing word is just its set of letters (sorted). A path is just its word of R's and U's.

Solution

  1. (a) Send each 4-letter word with strictly increasing letters to the set of its 4 letters. The inverse sends a 4-letter set to its letters in alphabetical order, which is the only increasing word with those letters.Why: a map with an inverse is a bijection.
  2. So (a) \(= \binom{26}{4} = \tfrac{26\cdot25\cdot24\cdot23}{24} = \) \(14950\).Why: count 4-element subsets of the 26 letters.
  3. (b) An up/right path from \((0,0)\) to \((4,3)\) makes exactly 4 right steps (R) and 3 up steps (U), 7 in total. Send it to its step word, e.g. RRURURU. The inverse follows the word.Why: \(x\) must grow by 4 and \(y\) by 3, one unit per step.
  4. So (b) = the number of 7-letter words with 3 U's \(= \binom73 = \) \(35\).Why: choose the positions of the 3 U's.

Conclusion. 14950 words and 35 paths. ∎

Chess club

A chess club has 100 members, and Alex is one of them.

Question: which is larger: (a) the number of 10-person committees that do not include Alex, or (b) the number of 11-person committees (with or without Alex)?

Key idea

Adding Alex maps (a) onto the 11-person committees that contain Alex, a proper subset of (b).

Picture

Add Alex to each 10-person committee. That gives exactly the 11-person committees that contain Alex. But there are also 11-person committees without Alex, so (b) is bigger.

Solution

Setup. 100 members including Alex. (a) = the 10-person committees without Alex. (b) = all 11-person committees.

Claim. (b) is larger.

  1. Adding Alex, \(X \mapsto X \cup \{\text{Alex}\}\), sends each committee in (a) to an 11-person committee that contains Alex.Why: \(X\) has 10 members, none of them Alex.
  2. This is a bijection onto the 11-person committees containing Alex.Why: the inverse removes Alex.
  3. (b) also contains 11-person committees without Alex (e.g. 11 of the other 99 members). So (b) is strictly bigger than (a).Why: (a) matches only part of (b) (steps 1–2), and the rest of (b) is non-empty.
  4. In numbers: \(|(a)| = \binom{99}{10}\) and \(|(b)| = \binom{100}{11} = \binom{99}{10} + \binom{99}{11} > \binom{99}{10}\).Why: Pascal: an 11-committee either contains Alex (\(\binom{99}{10}\)) or not (\(\binom{99}{11}\)).

Conclusion. There are more 11-person committees. ∎

Diagonal crossings in a 12-gon

Draw all the diagonals of a convex 12-sided polygon.

Question: what is the largest possible number of points inside the polygon where two diagonals cross?

Key idea

A crossing pair of diagonals ↔ its 4 endpoints. Any 4 vertices give exactly one crossing pair (the diagonals of their quadrilateral).

Picture

A crossing is decided by its 4 endpoints. Any 4 vertices form a quadrilateral, whose two diagonals cross exactly once. So there are as many crossings as ways to choose 4 vertices: C(12, 4).

Solution

Setup. A convex 12-gon; we count points inside it where two diagonals cross.

Claim. At most \(\binom{12}{4} = 495\).

  1. Any 4 vertices form a convex quadrilateral, and its two diagonals cross exactly once, inside.Why: vertices of a convex polygon are in convex position. Of the 3 ways to pair 4 points into 2 segments, the two pairs of opposite sides don't cross, and the pair of diagonals does.
  2. Two diagonals that cross inside the polygon have 4 different endpoints.Why: if they shared an endpoint, they would meet only there, on the boundary.
  3. So crossing pairs of diagonals match 4-element sets of vertices one-to-one: there are \(\binom{12}{4} = \tfrac{12\cdot11\cdot10\cdot9}{24} = 495\) of them.Why: steps 1 and 2 are the two directions of the match.
  4. The number of crossing points is at most the number of crossing pairs, with equality exactly when no three diagonals pass through one point.Why: a point where three or more diagonals meet is shared by several pairs.
  5. Equality can be achieved by moving the vertices slightly, keeping the polygon convex, so that no three diagonals meet at a point.Why: a small perturbation keeps each crossing (step 1) but breaks exact coincidences. (A regular 12-gon does have coincidences, e.g. 6 diameters through the centre.)

Conclusion. The maximum number of crossing points is 495. ∎

Even rows and columns

Colour each cell of an \(8\times8\) grid black or white so that every row and every column contains an even number of white cells.

Question: how many such colourings are there? Write the answer as \(2^a\).

Key idea

The top-left \(7\times7\) block is free. The last column and row are forced by parity, and the corner is consistent.

Picture

Fill the top-left 7 × 7 freely; everything else is forced. The last cell of each row fixes that row's parity; the last row fixes each column's. The corner works out either way (both equal the total parity of the 7 × 7 block).

Solution

Setup. Write 1 for white and 0 for black. The condition is that every row sum and every column sum of the \(8\times8\) grid is even.

Claim. \(2^{49}\) ways, so \(a = 49\).

  1. Fill the top-left \(7\times7\) block freely: \(2^{49}\) ways.Why: 49 cells, 2 colours each.
  2. For each row \(i \le 7\), the cell \((i, 8)\) is forced: it must be 1 if the first 7 cells of row \(i\) sum to an odd number, and 0 otherwise.Why: the row sum must be even.
  3. For each column \(j \le 7\), the cell \((8, j)\) is forced in the same way.Why: the column sum must be even.
  4. The corner \((8,8)\) is needed by row 8 and by column 8, and both ask for the same value.Why: row 8 needs the corner to have the parity of \((8,1) + \dots + (8,7)\). By step 3, each \((8,j)\) has the parity of column \(j\) of the block, so this sum has the parity of the whole block. Column 8 needs the parity of \((1,8) + \dots + (7,8)\), which by step 2 is also the parity of the whole block.
  5. So every filling of the block extends to exactly one valid grid, and every valid grid comes from its own top-left block.Why: steps 2–4 force everything outside the block, consistently.

Conclusion. Valid grids match the free \(7\times7\) blocks, so there are \(2^{49}\). ∎

14 · Reflection principle

  1. \(\pm1\) walks of \(n\) steps from 0 to \(d\): \(\binom{n}{(n+d)/2}\), when \(n+d\) is even.
  2. Reflection: suppose the walk ends on one side of a level \(m\) and must touch \(m\). Flip every step after the first visit to \(m\). This is a bijection with the walks ending at \(2m - \text{end}\). The inverse is the same flip, because the first visit to \(m\) is the same in both.

Walk counts

A walk on the number line starts at 0, and each step goes \(+1\) or \(-1\).

Question: count the walks (a) of 8 steps that end at 0; (b) of 8 steps that end at 2; (c) of 8 steps that end at 0 and go below 0 at some point; (d) of 10 steps that end at 4 and reach 6 at some point.

Key idea

(c) reflect at \(-1\), so the end becomes \(-2\). (d) reflect at 6, so the end becomes 8.

Picture

Reflection: to count walks that touch a level, flip everything after the first touch. Walks from 0 to 0 that dip to −1 become walks from 0 to −2, which are easy to count.

Solution

Setup. A walk takes steps \(+1\) or \(-1\). With \(u\) up-steps out of \(n\), it ends at \(u - (n - u) = 2u - n\). So the walks from 0 to \(d\) in \(n\) steps are those with \(u = \tfrac{n+d}{2}\), and there are \(\binom{n}{(n+d)/2}\) of them.

  1. (a) 8 steps, 0 to 0: \(u = 4\), \(\binom84 = \) \(70\). (b) 0 to 2: \(u = 5\), \(\binom85 = \) \(56\).Why: the setup formula.
  2. (c) A walk from 0 dips below 0 exactly when it visits \(-1\).Why: steps have size 1, so to go below 0 it must pass through \(-1\).
  3. Reflection: take a walk 0 → 0 that visits \(-1\), and flip every step after its first visit to \(-1\). The part after that visit went from \(-1\) to 0 (a change of \(+1\)); flipped, it changes by \(-1\), so the new walk ends at \(-2\).Why: flipping every step negates the total change of that part.
  4. This is a bijection between walks 0 → 0 that visit \(-1\) and all walks 0 → \(-2\).Why: every walk to \(-2\) visits \(-1\) (it starts above and ends below), and flipping after that same first visit undoes the map.
  5. So (c) = walks 0 → \(-2\) in 8 steps: \(u = 3\), \(\binom83 = \) \(56\).Why: the setup formula with \(d = -2\).
  6. (d) 10 steps, 0 → 4, touching 6: flip after the first visit to 6.
    The rest went from 6 to 4 (change \(-2\)); flipped, it changes by \(+2\), so the end becomes 8.
    Every walk 0 → 8 passes 6, so this is a bijection with walks 0 → 8: \(u = 9\), \(\binom{10}{9} = \) \(10\).Why: same reflection argument as steps 3–4 with the level 6.

Conclusion. 70, 56, 56 and 10 (all brute-force checked). ∎

Valid brackets (\(n = 4\))

A bracket string is valid if every '(' is closed by a later ')', for example (()())().

Question: how many valid strings use 4 '(' and 4 ')'?

Key idea

"(" \(= +1\) and ")" \(= -1\). Valid means the walk \(0\to0\) never goes below 0. Bad ones reflect to walks \(0\to-2\).

Picture

Brackets are walks: "(" = up, ")" = down. Valid = never below 0. Count all 70 strings, subtract the bad ones: a bad string dips to −1, and reflecting it makes a walk to −2 (56 of them). 70 − 56 = 14.

Solution

Setup. 4 pairs of brackets: strings of 4 '(' and 4 ')'. Read '(' as \(+1\) and ')' as \(-1\), so each string is a walk of 8 steps from 0 to 0. A string is valid when every prefix has at least as many '(' as ')', i.e. the walk never goes below 0.

  1. All strings: \(\binom84 = 70\).Why: choose the positions of the 4 '('.
  2. Invalid strings = walks 0 → 0 that visit \(-1\) = walks 0 → \(-2\) (by reflection, as in the previous problem) \(= \binom83 = 56\).Why: flip every step after the first visit to \(-1\).
  3. Valid \(= 70 - 56 = \) \(14\), the Catalan number \(C_4\).Why: complement.

Conclusion. There are 14 valid bracket strings. ∎
