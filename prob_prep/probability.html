1 · Basic terms

  1. Sample space \(\Omega\): the set of all outcomes. Event: a subset of \(\Omega\).
  2. \(A\cap B\): both happen. \(A\cup B\): at least one happens. \(A^c\): \(A\) does not happen.
  3. Mutually exclusive: \(A\cap B = \varnothing\). Exhaustive: \(A_1\cup\dots\cup A_n = \Omega\). A partition is both.
  4. Independent: \(P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)\) (see §6).

2 · Probability of an event

  1. If all outcomes are equally likely: \(P(A) = |A|/|\Omega|\). This formula only holds under that condition.
  2. \(P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)\).
  3. Trap: {red, blue} is not an equally likely sample space for 9 red + 1 blue balls. Label the balls 1–10 instead: \(P(\text{blue}) = \tfrac{1}{10}\).

Warm-ups

Question: (a) One card is drawn from a well-shuffled 52-card deck. What is the probability that it is a king? (b) A fair coin is flipped 3 times. What is the probability of getting exactly 2 heads?

Key idea

List outcomes so that each is equally likely.

Picture

List outcomes that are equally likely, then count. A card: 52 equally likely cards, 4 kings. Three flips: 8 equally likely sequences, 3 with exactly two H.

Solution

Setup. (a) One card from a shuffled 52-card deck: all 52 cards are equally likely. (b) Three fair flips: all \(2^3 = 8\) sequences HHH, HHT, …, TTT are equally likely.

  1. (a) There are 4 kings, so \(P(\text{king}) = \tfrac{4}{52} = \) \(\tfrac{1}{13} \approx 0.07692\).Why: favourable outcomes over all outcomes, valid because the 52 outcomes are equally likely.
  2. (b) Exactly 2 heads means choosing which 2 of the 3 flips are heads: \(\binom32 = 3\) sequences (HHT, HTH, THH). So \(P = \) \(\tfrac38\).Why: 3 favourable out of 8 equally likely sequences.

Conclusion. \(\tfrac{1}{13}\) and \(\tfrac38\). ∎

3 · Inclusion–exclusion

The same as counting PIE, with \(|\cdot|\) replaced by \(P(\cdot)\) (divide by \(|\Omega|\), or use additivity): \[P(A\cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A\cap B),\] \[P(A\cup B\cup C) = \sum P(A) - \sum P(A\cap B) + P(A\cap B\cap C).\]

Sports and drinks

(a) In a group of people, the probability that a person plays sport S is 0.7, plays sport B is 0.5, and plays both is 0.4.
(b) In another group, the probabilities that a person drinks:
  tea, coffee, juice: 0.6, 0.5, 0.4;
  tea and coffee, tea and juice, coffee and juice: 0.30, 0.20, 0.15;
  all three: 0.10.

Question: (a) what is the probability that a person plays at least one of the two sports? (b) What is the probability that a person drinks at least one of the three drinks?

Key idea

Plug into PIE.

Picture

Adding the circles counts the overlaps too often; inclusion–exclusion fixes that. Fill the Venn diagram from the centre out and the regions add to the answer.

Solution

  1. (a) \(P(S \cup B) = P(S) + P(B) - P(S \cap B) = .7 + .5 - .4 = \) \(0.8\).Why: inclusion–exclusion for two events: \(P(S) + P(B)\) counts \(S \cap B\) twice, so subtract it once.
  2. (b) With \(T, C, J\) for tea, coffee, juice: \(P(T \cup C \cup J) = (.6 + .5 + .4) - (.30 + .20 + .15) + .10\).Why: inclusion–exclusion for three events: add the singles, subtract the pairs, add back the triple.
  3. \(= 1.5 - .65 + .10 = \) \(0.95\).Why: arithmetic. The Venn diagram below shows the 7 regions adding to 0.95.

Conclusion. 0.8 and 0.95. ∎

4 · Counting in probability

Ask: can I count both all outcomes and the favourable ones, in an equally likely model? If so, \(P = \text{favourable}/\text{all}\).

A and B not adjacent

5 people, including A and B, stand in a row in a random order (all orders equally likely).

Question: what is the probability that A and B are not standing next to each other?

Key idea

Complement of the block count.

Picture

Count the opposite, then subtract. Glue A and B into one block: 4 units in 4! orders, times 2 inside the block = 48 of the 120 rows. So apart = 1 − 48/120.

Solution

Setup. 5 people in a random row: all \(5! = 120\) orders are equally likely.

  1. Rows with A and B adjacent: glue A and B into a block, giving 4 units in \(4! = 24\) orders, times 2 orders inside the block: \(48\).Why: each such row comes from exactly one order of the units and one order inside the block.
  2. \(P(\text{adjacent}) = \tfrac{48}{120} = \tfrac25\).Why: favourable over equally likely.
  3. \(P(\text{not adjacent}) = 1 - \tfrac25 = \) \(\tfrac35\).Why: complement.

Conclusion. A and B are apart with probability \(\tfrac35\). ∎

First HH exactly at toss 10

A fair coin is tossed until two heads in a row (HH) appear for the first time.

Question: what is the probability that this first HH is completed exactly on toss 10? Write it as a fraction \(p/q\) in lowest terms and report \(p + q\).

Picture

Remember one thing: was the last toss H? Go toss by toss. An H after an H ends the game (a win only if it is toss 10). The target toss is fixed, so the answer depends on which toss you are at: fill a table from toss 10 backwards.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one toss. State h = was the previous toss H? HH → ends. There is a fixed deadline (toss 10), so MEMORYLESS keeps i.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i, h) = P(the first HH is completed exactly on toss 10), given tosses 1 to i−1 had no HH, and h = 1 if toss i−1 was H (h = 0 if it was T, or if i = 1). The answer is rec(1, 0).Why: to know whether toss i completes an HH you must remember only whether toss i−1 was H.
  2. CHOICE. Toss i is H or T, each \(\tfrac12\).Why: a fair coin.
  3. CHECK. H with h = 1: HH is completed on toss i, ends, value 1 if i = 10 and 0 otherwise. H with h = 0: continue to (i+1, 1). T: continue to (i+1, 0). If i = 11, toss 10 has passed with no HH: value 0.Why: the definition of 'first HH exactly on toss 10'.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i, 1) = \tfrac12\,[i = 10] + \tfrac12\,\text{rec}(i+1, 0)\). \(\text{rec}(i, 0) = \tfrac12\,\text{rec}(i+1, 1) + \tfrac12\,\text{rec}(i+1, 0)\).Why: law of total probability over toss i.
  5. MEMORYLESS (check it). No: the coin forgets, but the target 'toss 10' is fixed, so from toss i+1 there is one toss less to go. rec(i+1, h) ≠ rec(i, h); keep i. Since i only grows, the recursion stops at the base case i = 11: plain recursion, no equation to solve.Why: a fixed deadline makes the turn number part of the state.

\(3\times2\) matrix, rows and columns \(\equiv 0 \pmod 3\)

Each of the 6 entries of a \(3\times2\) matrix (3 rows, 2 columns) is chosen independently and uniformly from \(\{0, 1, 2\}\).

Question: what is the probability that every row sum and every column sum is divisible by 3?

Key idea

The two top-left entries determine everything.

Picture

Count the free choices. Once the first column's top two entries are chosen, every other entry is forced by the "multiple of 3" rules, and the last column checks itself. 9 free choices out of 729.

Solution

Setup. A \(3\times2\) matrix \((a_{ij})\) with each entry uniform on \(\{0, 1, 2\}\), independently. All \(3^6 = 729\) matrices are equally likely. We want every row sum and every column sum to be \(\equiv 0 \pmod 3\).

  1. Each row \(i\) needs \(a_{i1} + a_{i2} \equiv 0\), i.e. \(a_{i2} \equiv -a_{i1}\). So \(a_{i2}\) is determined by \(a_{i1}\).Why: for each value of \(a_{i1}\) exactly one value in \(\{0,1,2\}\) works.
  2. Column 1 needs \(a_{11} + a_{21} + a_{31} \equiv 0\), so \(a_{31}\) is determined by \(a_{11}\) and \(a_{21}\).Why: same reason.
  3. Column 2 is then automatically fine: \(a_{12} + a_{22} + a_{32} \equiv -(a_{11} + a_{21} + a_{31}) \equiv 0\).Why: step 1 gives each \(a_{i2} \equiv -a_{i1}\), and step 2 gives the column 1 sum \(\equiv 0\).
  4. So the valid matrices match the free choices of \((a_{11}, a_{21})\): \(3 \cdot 3 = 9\) of them.Why: everything else is forced (steps 1–3), and every choice of the two free entries works.
  5. \(P = \tfrac{9}{729} = \) \(\tfrac{1}{81} \approx 0.01235\).Why: favourable over equally likely.

Conclusion. The probability is \(\tfrac{1}{81}\). ∎

Partial sums of 1..13 never \(\equiv 0 \pmod 3\)

The numbers \(1, 2, \dots, 13\) are put in a random order. Reading from left to right, write down the running total after each number (13 running totals in all).

Question: what is the probability that none of the 13 running totals is divisible by 3? Write it as \(p/q\) in lowest terms and report \(p + q\).

Picture

Only remainders mod 3 matter. Multiples of 3 never change the running total (they just can't go first). The other nine numbers are forced: after a total of 1 you must add a 1-type, after 2 a 2-type — the pattern 1, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2. Count those orders and divide by 13!.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Level = one position of the order. State = how many numbers of each residue are left, and the running total mod 3. Count the good orders, then divide by 13!.

  1. LEVEL. Sort 1..13 by residue mod 3:
      five are ≡ 1 (1, 4, 7, 10, 13), four are ≡ 2 (2, 5, 8, 11), four are ≡ 0 (3, 6, 9, 12).
    rec(a, b, z, s) = the number of ways to order the numbers still left (a of residue 1, b of residue 2, z of residue 0) so that no running total is ≡ 0, given the running total so far is ≡ s.
    The answer is rec(5, 4, 4, 0) / 13!.Why: to check the next running total we only need the current total mod 3 and which residues are still available.
  2. CHOICE. The next number: one of the a numbers ≡ 1, one of the b numbers ≡ 2, or one of the z numbers ≡ 0.Why: any number still left can go next.
  3. CHECK. The new running total s + residue must not be ≡ 0 (mod 3). All numbers used: base case, 1 way.Why: the event.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(a,b,z,s) = a\cdot\text{rec}(a{-}1,b,z,s{+}1) + b\cdot\text{rec}(a,b{-}1,z,s{+}2) + z\cdot\text{rec}(a,b,z{-}1,s)\), keeping only the terms that pass CHECK (residues taken mod 3).Why: sum rule over the next number; the factor a (or b, z) counts which actual number of that residue goes next.
  5. SOLVE (code). 
    ll dp[6][5][5][3];                              // all -1
ll rec(ll a, ll b, ll z, ll s) {
    if (a == 0 && b == 0 && z == 0) return 1;   // base case: all placed
    if (dp[a][b][z][s] != -1) return dp[a][b][z][s];   // cache check
    ll ans = 0;
    if (a && (s + 1) % 3) ans += a * rec(a - 1, b, z, (s + 1) % 3);
    if (b && (s + 2) % 3) ans += b * rec(a, b - 1, z, (s + 2) % 3);
    if (z && s % 3)       ans += z * rec(a, b, z - 1, s);
    return dp[a][b][z][s] = ans;                // save and return
}   // rec(5, 4, 4, 0) = 34214400    cost: 6*5*5*3 states x 3 transitions
    rec(5, 4, 4, 0) = 34 214 400, and \(\tfrac{34\,214\,400}{13!} = \tfrac{34\,214\,400}{6\,227\,020\,800} = \tfrac{1}{182}\).Why: 6 227 020 800 / 34 214 400 = 182.
  6. BY HAND (why the count is 5!·4!·9·10·11·12).
    Zeros: a 0-residue never changes the total, so it can go anywhere except first.
    Nonzeros: from total s ∈ {1, 2} the next nonzero residue must equal s (the other would make 0). So the 9 of them are forced to be 1, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2 (starting with 2 would need five 2s; there are only four).
    Count: the 1s fill their slots in 5! ways, the 2s in 4! ways, and 3, 6, 9, 12 are inserted one at a time never in front: 9, 10, 11, 12 places.Why: this is the CHECK read by hand: it leaves only one residue pattern.

Answer. \(P = \tfrac{1}{182}\), \(p + q = \) \(183\). ∎

5 · Conditional probability

  1. \(\displaystyle P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\), for \(P(B) > 0\). When outcomes are equally likely, this is \(|A\cap B|/|B|\): the sample space shrinks to \(B\).
  2. The template for probability = Form 1 of the DP template: LEVEL → CHOICE → CHECK → MOVE → MEMORYLESS → SOLVE. For any process that repeats a turn until something ends it:
    1. LEVEL: rec(i, s) = probability / expected value from turn i onward, given state s before turn i. s = whatever you must remember (a score, a suffix, a position); leave it out when nothing carries over.
    2. CHOICE: every outcome of turn i, with its probability.
    3. CHECK: for each outcome: does the process end (value known), or continue to turn i+1 in state s′?
    4. MOVE: write the honest equation first: ending outcome → prob × value; continuing outcome → prob × rec(i+1, s′) (plus prob × cost, for an expectation).
    5. MEMORYLESS (only now): is the future from (i+1, s) exactly the future from (i, s)?
      Yes → drop i and write rec(s): equations to solve; a rec on both sides (self-loop) is solved by algebra.
      No, fixed deadline ("after exactly 6 moves") → keep i; i only grows, so it is plain recursion down to the deadline.
      No, for another reason → work out how they differ (HH ends on an even toss).
    6. SOLVE: line by line. No loops left → a C++ rec.
  3. "A before B" lemma. Independent identical turns. Each turn: \(A\) with prob \(a\) (end, value 1), \(B\) with prob \(b\) (end, value 0), else continue.
    MOVE: \(\text{rec}(i) = a + (1-a-b)\,\text{rec}(i+1)\).
    MEMORYLESS: \(\text{rec}(i+1) = \text{rec}(i) = x\).
    SOLVE: \((a+b)x = a\), so\[P(A \text{ first}) = \frac{a}{a+b}.\]

Trigger: "given…", or "one thing before another".

Red → diamond; even → \(>3\)

(a) One card is drawn from a shuffled 52-card deck. (b) A fair die is rolled.

Question: (a) find \(P(\text{diamond})\) and \(P(\text{diamond} \mid \text{the card is red})\), and add them. (b) Given that the die shows an even number, what is the probability that it is greater than 3?

Key idea

Shrink the sample space to the condition.

Picture

"Given" shrinks the sample space. Given red, only the 26 red cards are left, and 13 of them are diamonds. Given even, only 2, 4, 6 are left, and 4, 6 are above 3.

Solution

Setup. (a) One card from a shuffled deck. (b) One roll of a fair die.

  1. (a) \(P(\diamondsuit) = \tfrac{13}{52} = \tfrac14\).Why: 13 diamonds among 52 equally likely cards.
  2. \(P(\diamondsuit \mid \text{red}) = \dfrac{|\diamondsuit \cap \text{red}|}{|\text{red}|} = \dfrac{13}{26} = \dfrac12\). Their sum is \(0.75\).Why: given red, the sample space shrinks to the 26 red cards, equally likely, and all 13 diamonds are red.
  3. (b) \(P(>3 \mid \text{even}) = \dfrac{|\{4, 6\}|}{|\{2, 4, 6\}|} = \) \(\tfrac23\).Why: given even, the sample space is \(\{2, 4, 6\}\), equally likely; the ones above 3 are 4 and 6.

Conclusion. 0.75 and \(\tfrac23\). ∎

First ace at 27, then 2♥?

A standard 52-card deck is shuffled and dealt face up one card at a time. Suppose you are told that the first ace to appear is the 27th card.

Question: given this, what is the probability that the 28th card is the 2 of hearts?

Key idea

Given the condition, the first 26 cards are a uniform choice of non-aces, and the 25 cards after position 27 are in uniform random order.

Picture

Picture the condition. The first 26 cards are non-aces, card 27 is an ace, and the remaining 25 cards are in random order. 2♥ is among those 25 with chance 22/48, and then sits at spot 28 with chance 1/25.

Solution

Setup. A shuffled 52-card deck; all \(52!\) orders are equally likely. \(E\) = 'the first ace is at position 27'. We want \(P(\text{position 28 is } 2\heartsuit \mid E)\).

  1. Given \(E\): positions 1–26 hold 26 of the 48 non-aces, position 27 holds an ace, and positions 28–52 hold the remaining 25 cards (3 aces and 22 non-aces).Why: that is what 'first ace at 27' means.
  2. Given \(E\), each non-ace is equally likely to be among the first 26, and the 25 cards after position 27 are in uniformly random order.Why: swapping two non-aces everywhere, or rearranging positions 28–52, maps orders in \(E\) to orders in \(E\) one-to-one, and all orders are equally likely.
  3. \(P(2\heartsuit \text{ is not in positions 1–26} \mid E) = \tfrac{22}{48}\).Why: 26 of the 48 non-aces are in the first 26 positions, each non-ace equally likely to be one of them (step 2); so \(2\heartsuit\) is there with probability \(\tfrac{26}{48}\) and not there with \(\tfrac{22}{48}\).
  4. If \(2\heartsuit\) is among the last 25 cards, it is at position 28 with probability \(\tfrac{1}{25}\).Why: uniform order of those 25 cards (step 2).
  5. \(P = \tfrac{22}{48}\cdot\tfrac{1}{25} = \tfrac{22}{1200} = \) \(\tfrac{11}{600} \approx 0.018\).Why: multiplication rule: steps 3 and 4.

Conclusion. The probability is \(\tfrac{11}{600}\). ∎

Robot clicks H

The 26 letters A to Z are written in a row. A robot repeatedly chooses one of the letters still present, uniformly at random, and clicks it: the clicked letter and every letter to its right are deleted. This goes on until no letters are left.

Question: what is the probability that the letter H gets clicked at some point?

Picture

Only the letters A to H matter. Clicks to the right of H just shorten the row. The first click that lands on A…H decides everything: H is clicked exactly if that click is H itself, and each of the 8 letters is equally likely.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one click. State n = letters still present (A up to the n-th letter). Click H → ends (1). Click left of H → ends (0, H is deleted). Click right of H → next turn with fewer letters.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i, n) = P(H gets clicked at some point), given that before click i the letters present are the first n letters (A, B, …) with n ≥ 8, so H (the 8th letter) is still there. The answer is rec(1, 26).Why: a click deletes a letter and everything to its right, so what is left is always A up to some letter.
  2. CHOICE. Click letter k, for each k in {1, …, n}, with probability \(\tfrac1n\).Why: each click is uniform over the letters present.
  3. CHECK. k = 8 (H): ends, value 1. k ≤ 7: H is to the right of k, so it is deleted: ends, value 0. k ≥ 9: letters 1 to k−1 remain, continue to (i+1, k−1).Why: the rules.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i, n) = \tfrac1n\cdot 1 + \sum_{k=9}^{n}\tfrac1n\,\text{rec}(i{+}1, k{-}1) = \tfrac1n\Big(1 + \sum_{m=8}^{n-1}\text{rec}(i{+}1, m)\Big)\).Why: law of total probability over the click; put m = k − 1.
  5. MEMORYLESS. rec(i+1, n) = rec(i, n) = rec(n).Why: the rules at a turn depend only on the state s, never on the turn number i, and the coin/die/walk has no memory. So the future from (i+1, s) is exactly the future from (i, s).
  6. SOLVE. rec(8) = \(\tfrac18\) (the sum is empty). If rec(8), …, rec(n−1) are all \(\tfrac18\), then \(\text{rec}(n) = \tfrac1n\big(1 + \tfrac{n-8}{8}\big) = \tfrac1n\cdot\tfrac n8 = \tfrac18\). So rec(26) = \(\tfrac18\).Why: there are n − 8 terms in the sum; n only goes down, so build up from n = 8. (Same shape as the Falling frog.)

Answer. H is clicked with probability \(\tfrac18\). ∎

⚠ Course's “1 way to click H / 8 ways to remove it” is only valid because every click is uniform over the letters present, which it never states.

7 before 11

Two dice are rolled over and over. If the total is 7 you win; if the total is 11 you lose; any other total means you roll again.

Question: what is the probability that you win?

Picture

Ignore the rolls that change nothing. Only 7 (6 ways) and 11 (2 ways) end the game, so the game ends on a 7 with chance 6 ÷ (6 + 2) = ¾.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one roll of two dice. 7 → ends (win). 11 → ends (lose). Anything else → next turn.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i) = P(you win, looking from turn i onward), given that turns 1 to i−1 were neither 7 nor 11. The answer is rec(1).Why: the game is a sequence of turns; rec(i) is the probability looking forward from turn i.
  2. CHOICE. At turn i the total is 7 with probability \(\tfrac{6}{36}\), 11 with probability \(\tfrac{2}{36}\), neither with probability \(1 - \tfrac{6}{36} - \tfrac{2}{36} = \tfrac{28}{36}\).Why: 7 comes from (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1): 6 of the 36 equally likely pairs. 11 comes from (5,6), (6,5): 2 pairs. The three cases are disjoint and cover every roll.
  3. CHECK. 7: ends, you win, value 1. 11: ends, you lose, value 0. Neither: continue to turn i+1.Why: the rules.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i) = \tfrac{6}{36}\cdot 1 + \tfrac{2}{36}\cdot 0 + \tfrac{28}{36}\,\text{rec}(i+1)\).Why: law of total probability over the outcome of turn i. A continuing outcome contributes (its probability) × rec(i+1).
  5. MEMORYLESS. At turn i+1 the next roll is again 7 with \(\tfrac{6}{36}\), 11 with \(\tfrac{2}{36}\), neither with \(\tfrac{28}{36}\), under the same rules. So \(\text{rec}(i+1) = \text{rec}(i)\).Why: each turn is independent of earlier turns, has the same probabilities and the same rules, and nothing from earlier turns is carried forward.
  6. SOLVE. Let \(x = \text{rec}(i)\). \(x = \tfrac{6}{36} + \tfrac{28}{36}x\). So \(x - \tfrac{28}{36}x = \tfrac{6}{36}\), which is \(\tfrac{8}{36}x = \tfrac{6}{36}\), so \(x = \tfrac68 = \) \(\tfrac34\).Why: substitute MEMORYLESS into MOVE; move the x terms left; divide by \(\tfrac{8}{36}\).

Answer. You win with probability \(\tfrac34\). (The 'A before B' lemma: \(\tfrac{a}{a+b}\) with \(a = \tfrac{6}{36}\), \(b = \tfrac{2}{36}\).) ∎

6 · Multiplication rule & independence

  1. \(P(A\cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\). Chained: \[P(A_1\cap\dots\cap A_n) = P(A_1)\,P(A_2 \mid A_1)\cdots P(A_n \mid A_1\cap\dots\cap A_{n-1}).\]
  2. Independent \(A, B\): \(P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)\), equivalently \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\) when \(P(B) > 0\).
  3. Mutually independent \(A_1,\dots,A_n\): the product rule holds for every sub-collection, not just all \(n\) together.
  4. If \(A, B\) are independent, so are \(A^c, B^c\): \(P(A^c\cap B^c) = 1 - P(A) - P(B) + P(A)P(B) = (1-P(A))(1-P(B))\).

⚠ Course defines independence of \(n\) events by the single equation \(P(\bigcap A_i) = \prod P(A_i)\). That is not enough. Counterexample: \(A = B\), \(P(A) = \tfrac12\), \(C = \varnothing\); the full product holds (\(0 = 0\)), but \(A\) and \(B\) are not independent.

Draws without replacement

Balls or cards are drawn one after another without replacement (a drawn item is not put back).

Question:
(a) A bag has 5 green and 3 red balls, and 2 are drawn. What is the probability that the first is red and the second is green?
(b) Two cards are drawn from a 52-card deck. What is the probability that the first is an ace and the second is a queen?
(c) Three cards are drawn. What is the probability of an ace, then a queen, then a king?

Key idea

Chain rule: each factor is computed on the remaining deck.

Picture

Multiply along the path, updating the deck each time. First red: 3/8. Then 7 balls are left and 5 are green: 5/7.

Solution

  1. (a) 5 green, 3 red. \(P(\text{1st red}) = \tfrac38\). Given that, 7 balls remain and 5 are green: \(P(\text{2nd green} \mid \text{1st red}) = \tfrac57\). Multiply: \(\tfrac{15}{56} \approx 0.26786\).Why: chain rule \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A)\); each factor is computed on what is left.
  2. (b) \(P(\text{ace}) = \tfrac{4}{52}\), then \(P(\text{queen} \mid \text{ace first}) = \tfrac{4}{51}\). Product: \(\tfrac{16}{2652} = \) \(\tfrac{4}{663} \approx 0.00603\).Why: after one card, 51 remain and all 4 queens are still there.
  3. (c) Then \(P(\text{king} \mid \text{ace, queen}) = \tfrac{4}{50}\). Product: \(\tfrac{4}{52}\cdot\tfrac{4}{51}\cdot\tfrac{4}{50} = \tfrac{64}{132600} \approx\) \(0.00048\).Why: 50 cards remain, all 4 kings among them.

Conclusion. \(\tfrac{15}{56}\), \(\tfrac{4}{663}\) and about 0.00048. ∎

Ant on an icosahedron

An icosahedron is a solid with 12 corners (vertices); each vertex is joined by edges to 5 others. An ant starts at vertex A. At each move it walks along one of the 5 edges at its current vertex, chosen at random. B is the vertex directly opposite A.

Question: what is the probability that the ant is at B after exactly 3 moves?

Picture

Only the distance from A matters, not which vertex. Group the 12 vertices into 4 levels (A, its 5 neighbours, B's 5 neighbours, B) and follow the level for exactly 3 moves.

To reach B in 3 moves the ant must go down a level every time: 1 × 2/5 × 1/5.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one move. State L = level (distance from A). Fixed deadline (3 moves), so MEMORYLESS keeps i.

  1. LEVEL. Group the vertices by distance from A:
      level 0 = A; level 1 = the 5 neighbours of A; level 2 = the 5 neighbours of B; level 3 = B.
    rec(i, L) = P(ant is at B after move 3), given it is at level L after i moves. The answer is rec(0, 0).Why: 12 = 1 + 5 + 5 + 1 vertices; only the level matters for where the ant can go next.
  2. CHOICE. From level 0: all 5 edges go to level 1. From level 1: 1 edge to level 0, 2 inside level 1, 2 to level 2. From level 2: 2 to level 1, 2 inside level 2, 1 to level 3. Each edge has probability \(\tfrac15\).Why: the neighbours of A form a ring of 5, and so do the neighbours of B; the edge counts add up to 5 at every vertex.
  3. CHECK. i = 3: the walk is over, value 1 if L = 3 and 0 otherwise. i < 3: continue to (i+1, L′).Why: the question asks about the position after exactly 3 moves.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i, L) = \sum_{L'} P(L \to L')\,\text{rec}(i+1, L')\).Why: law of total probability over the move.
  5. MEMORYLESS (check it). No: the deadline 'after 3 moves' is fixed, so keep i. i only grows, so this is plain recursion down to i = 3.Why: a fixed deadline makes the turn number part of the state.
  6. SOLVE. rec(3, L) = [L = 3]. rec(2, L) = P(L → 3): \(\tfrac15\) for L = 2, 0 otherwise. rec(1, 1) = P(1 → 2)·\(\tfrac15\) = \(\tfrac25\cdot\tfrac15 = \tfrac{2}{25}\). rec(0, 0) = P(0 → 1)·rec(1, 1) = \(1\cdot\tfrac{2}{25} = \) \(\tfrac{2}{25}\).Why: MOVE, read backwards from the deadline; from level 0 the ant surely reaches level 1.

Answer. The ant is at B after 3 moves with probability \(\tfrac{2}{25}\). ∎

Shooting stars

(a) The probability of seeing a shooting star in any given hour is 0.6, and different hours are independent. (b) The probability of seeing at least one shooting star in an hour is 0.8. Think of the hour as four 15-minute blocks that are independent and alike.

Question: (a) what is the probability of seeing at least one shooting star in 2 hours? (b) What is the probability of seeing a star in the first 15 minutes?

Key idea

Work with "none": the complements multiply.

Picture

Work with "no star at all": independent pieces multiply. Two hours with no star: 0.4 × 0.4. An hour with no star is four quarter-hours with no star: (1 − p)⁴ = 0.2.

Solution

  1. (a) \(P(\text{no star in one hour}) = 1 - 0.6 = 0.4\), so \(P(\text{no star in 2 hours}) = 0.4^2 = 0.16\).Why: complement, then the two hours are independent, so the 'none' probabilities multiply.
  2. \(P(\text{at least one in 2 hours}) = 1 - 0.16 = \) \(0.84\).Why: complement.
  3. (b) Let \(p = P(\text{star in one 15-min block})\). The hour is 4 independent identical blocks, so \(P(\text{no star in the hour}) = (1 - p)^4\).Why: no star in the hour means none in each of the 4 blocks.
  4. \((1 - p)^4 = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2\), so \(1 - p = 0.2^{1/4} \approx 0.66874\) and \(p \approx\) \(0.33126\).Why: take the fourth root: \(\sqrt{0.2} \approx 0.44721\) and \(\sqrt{0.44721} \approx 0.66874\).

Conclusion. 0.84 and about 0.331. ∎

7 · Law of total probability

If \(A_1,\dots,A_n\) partition \(\Omega\), then \(B = \bigcup (B\cap A_i)\), a disjoint union. So \[P(B) = \sum_i P(B \mid A_i)\,P(A_i).\] In practice: list the disjoint ways \(B\) can happen and add them up.

Defects and coins

(a) Machine A makes 60% of a factory's items and machine B makes the other 40%. 2% of A's items are defective and 5% of B's items are defective. (b) There are three coins whose probabilities of heads are 0.5, 0.75 and 1. One coin is picked at random and flipped.

Split on which machine or coin.

Picture

Split by where the item came from, then add the branches. Defective from A: 0.6 × 0.02; defective from B: 0.4 × 0.05.

Solution

  1. (a) Every item comes from machine A (0.6) or B (0.4), and never both. So \(P(D) = P(D \mid A)P(A) + P(D \mid B)P(B)\).Why: law of total probability over the partition \(\{A, B\}\).

8 · Bayes

\[P(A_i \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_i)\,P(A_i)}{\sum_j P(B \mid A_j)\,P(A_j)}.\] This is just \(P(A_i\cap B)/P(B)\), with the numerator from the chain rule and the denominator from total probability.

Three bags

Bag 1 has 3 red and 4 black balls, bag 2 has 2 red and 11 black, and bag 3 has 8 red and 1 black. A bag is chosen at random (each with probability \(\tfrac13\)), and one ball is drawn from it. The ball is red.

Question: what is the probability that it came from bag 3?

Key idea

The equal priors cancel: the answer is the ratio of the likelihoods.

Picture

Among all the ways to draw red, what share goes through bag 3? The bags are equally likely, so compare the chances of red: 8/9 against 3/7 + 2/13 + 8/9.

Solution

Setup. A bag is picked uniformly (\(\tfrac13\) each), then a ball from it. \(P(\text{red} \mid \text{bag 1}) = \tfrac37\), \(P(\text{red} \mid \text{bag 2}) = \tfrac{2}{13}\), \(P(\text{red} \mid \text{bag 3}) = \tfrac89\).

  1. Bayes: \(P(\text{bag 3} \mid \text{red}) = \dfrac{P(\text{red} \mid 3)\,P(3)}{\sum_k P(\text{red} \mid k)\,P(k)}\).Why: numerator from the chain rule, denominator from total probability.
  2. The priors are all \(\tfrac13\) and cancel: \(= \dfrac{8/9}{3/7 + 2/13 + 8/9}\).Why: factor \(\tfrac13\) out of the numerator and every denominator term.
  3. Over the common denominator 819: \(\tfrac37 = \tfrac{351}{819}\), \(\tfrac{2}{13} = \tfrac{126}{819}\), \(\tfrac89 = \tfrac{728}{819}\). So \(P = \tfrac{728}{351 + 126 + 728} = \tfrac{728}{1205} \approx\) \(0.604\).Why: \(819 = 7\cdot9\cdot13\).

Conclusion. Given red, bag 3 has probability about 0.604. ∎

9 fair + 1 double-headed coin

A box holds 10 coins: 9 are fair and 1 has heads on both sides. A coin is picked at random and flipped, and it shows heads.

Question: what is the probability that it is the two-headed coin?

Key idea

Prior times likelihood, then normalise.

Picture

Among all the ways to see heads, what share uses the trick coin? Trick coin and heads: 1/10. Fair coin and heads: 9/10 × 1/2. So 1/10 ÷ (1/10 + 9/20).

Solution

Setup. 10 coins: 9 fair and 1 double-headed (DH). One is picked uniformly and flipped; it shows H.

  1. \(P(\text{DH and H}) = \tfrac{1}{10}\cdot1 = \tfrac{1}{10}\).Why: pick DH with probability \(\tfrac{1}{10}\); it always shows H.
  2. \(P(\text{fair and H}) = \tfrac{9}{10}\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac{9}{20}\).Why: pick a fair coin with probability \(\tfrac{9}{10}\), then H with \(\tfrac12\).
  3. \(P(H) = \tfrac{1}{10} + \tfrac{9}{20} = \tfrac{2}{20} + \tfrac{9}{20} = \tfrac{11}{20}\).Why: the two cases are disjoint and cover every way to get H.
  4. \(P(\text{DH} \mid H) = \dfrac{1/10}{11/20} = \dfrac{2}{11} \approx\) \(0.182\).Why: definition of conditional probability.

Conclusion. The coin is double-headed with probability \(\tfrac{2}{11}\). ∎

Disease test

1% of people have a certain disease. A test for it is positive for 90% of people who have the disease, but it is also (wrongly) positive for 5% of people who don't. A randomly chosen person tests positive.

Question: what is the probability that this person actually has the disease?

Key idea

The rare disease makes false positives outnumber true positives.

Picture

Picture 1000 people. 10 are sick and 9 of them test positive. 990 are healthy and about 50 of them test positive anyway. So of ~59 positives only 9 are sick: about 15%.

Solution

Setup. \(D\) = has the disease, \(T\) = tests positive. \(P(D) = 0.01\), \(P(T \mid D) = 0.9\), \(P(T \mid \text{not } D) = 0.05\).

  1. \(P(D \cap T) = 0.01 \cdot 0.9 = 0.009\).Why: chain rule.
  2. \(P(\text{not } D \cap T) = 0.99 \cdot 0.05 = 0.0495\).Why: chain rule; \(P(\text{not } D) = 0.99\).
  3. \(P(T) = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585\).Why: a positive test comes from a sick or a healthy person, never both.
  4. \(P(D \mid T) = \dfrac{0.009}{0.0585} \approx\) \(0.154\).Why: definition of conditional probability. In counts: of 10 000 people, 90 sick people test positive but so do 495 healthy people.

Conclusion. A positive test means disease with probability only about 0.154. ∎

  1. Start from the full, equally likely sample space. Delete what the information rules out. Only then count.
  2. Paths or stories are not equally likely by default: multiply the probabilities along each one.
  3. If someone chooses what to reveal, model how they choose. That rule is part of the information.

Three coins (HH, HT, TT)

A bag holds three coins: one with heads on both sides (HH), one with heads and tails (HT), and one with tails on both sides (TT). You pick a coin at random and look at one random side of it. That side is heads.

Question: what is the probability that you picked the HH coin (so the other side is also heads)?

Key idea

Count faces, not coins: there are 3 equally likely heads-faces, and 2 of them belong to HH.

Picture

Count faces, not coins. There are 3 heads-faces you could be looking at, all equally likely. Two of them are on the HH coin.

(The tempting ½ forgets that the HH coin shows heads twice as often as the HT coin.)

Solution

Setup. Three coins: HH (heads on both faces), HT, TT. A coin is picked uniformly, then one of its faces uniformly, and that face shows H.

  1. Name the faces: HH coin \(A_1, A_2\) (both H); HT coin \(B_1\) (H), \(B_2\) (T); TT coin \(C_1, C_2\) (T). Each of the 6 (coin, face) outcomes has probability \(\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac16\).Why: the coin is uniform, then the face is uniform.
  2. 'The face shows H' is the event \(\{A_1, A_2, B_1\}\): 3 equally likely outcomes.Why: these are the heads faces.
  3. Two of them, \(A_1\) and \(A_2\), are on the HH coin, so \(P = \) \(\tfrac23\).Why: conditioning on an event made of equally likely outcomes: count within it.
  4. The tempting answer \(\tfrac12\) is wrong because it treats the HH and HT coins as equally likely after seeing H. But the HH coin shows H every time and the HT coin only half the time.Why: the observation carries information about the coin.

Conclusion. Given a head, the coin is double-headed with probability \(\tfrac23\). ∎

Each child is a boy or a girl with probability \(\tfrac12\), independently of the others.

Question:
(a) A family has 2 children, and you learn that at least one is a girl. What is the probability that both are girls?
(b) Instead you learn that the older child is a girl. Now what is the probability that both are girls?
(c) 3 fair coins are tossed and at least one shows heads. What is the probability that all three show heads?

Remove the ruled-out outcomes from the equally likely list.

Picture

List the equal-chance families, cross out what the information rules out, then count. "At least one girl" rules out only BB; "the older is a girl" rules out BB and BG.

Solution

Setup. Two children, each independently a boy or girl with probability \(\tfrac12\). Writing (older, younger), the outcomes BB, BG, GB, GG are equally likely.

  3. (c) Three coins have 8 equally likely outcomes. 'At least one H' removes only TTT, leaving 7, of which 1 is HHH: \(\tfrac17 \approx 0.143\).Why: same method.

Falling frog

A frog sits on step 10 of a staircase with steps numbered 1 to 10. From step \(i\) (with \(i \ge 2\)) it jumps down to a step chosen uniformly at random from \(1, 2, \dots, i-1\). It stops when it reaches step 1.

Question: what is the probability that the frog lands on step 5 at some point?

Picture

Only the first landing below 6 matters. Jumps that land on 6 or higher just start again from a lower step. The first time the frog lands on one of the steps 1–5, each of those 5 steps is equally likely. So 1/5.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one jump. State j = current step. Landing on 5 → ends (1). Landing below 5 → ends (0). Landing on 6 or higher → next turn.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i, j) = P(the frog lands on 5 at some point from now on), given that after i jumps it is on step j ≥ 6. The answer is rec(0, 10).Why: the frog starts on 10 after 0 jumps.
  2. CHOICE. From step j it lands on k, for each k in {1, …, j−1}, with probability \(\tfrac{1}{j-1}\).Why: the rules.
  3. CHECK. k = 5: ends, value 1. k ≤ 4: value 0. k ≥ 6: continue to (i+1, k).Why: positions only go down, so from k ≤ 4 the frog can never reach 5.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i, j) = \tfrac{1}{j-1}\cdot 1 + \sum_{k=6}^{j-1}\tfrac{1}{j-1}\,\text{rec}(i+1, k)\).Why: law of total probability over the jump.
  5. MEMORYLESS. The jump rule depends only on the current step j, not on how many jumps came before, so rec(i+1, j) = rec(i, j) = rec(j).Why: the rules at a turn depend only on the state s, never on the turn number i, and the coin/die/walk has no memory. So the future from (i+1, s) is exactly the future from (i, s).
  6. SOLVE. \(\text{rec}(6) = \tfrac15\) (the sum is empty). \(\text{rec}(7) = \tfrac16\big(1 + \tfrac15\big) = \tfrac16\cdot\tfrac65 = \tfrac15\). \(\text{rec}(8) = \tfrac17\big(1 + \tfrac15 + \tfrac15\big) = \tfrac17\cdot\tfrac75 = \tfrac15\). In general, if rec(6), …, rec(j−1) are all \(\tfrac15\), then \(\text{rec}(j) = \tfrac{1}{j-1}\big(1 + \tfrac{j-6}{5}\big) = \tfrac{1}{j-1}\cdot\tfrac{j-1}{5} = \tfrac15\). So rec(10) = \(\tfrac15\).

double rec(ll j) {                              // j >= 6
    if (dp[j] != -1) return dp[j];              // cache check
    double ans = 1;                             // k = 5: lands on 5
    for (ll k = 6; k < j; k++) ans += rec(k);   // k >= 6: keeps falling
    return dp[j] = ans / (j - 1);               // save and return
}   // rec(10)      (j = 6: the loop is empty, that is the base case)
    Why: steps only go down, so there are no loops: build up from j = 6. There are j − 6 terms in the sum.

Answer. The frog lands on 5 with probability \(\tfrac15\). ∎

Three prisoners

Prisoners A, B and C know that two of them, chosen at random, will be released.
A asks the warden to name one of the other two who will be released. So the warden names B or C; if both B and C are being released, he picks one of them at random.
He says “B”.

Question: given this, what is the probability that A will be released?

Key idea

AB forces "B", but BC says "B" only half the time.

Picture

The warden's rule matters. If AB go free he must say "B". If BC go free he says "B" only half the time.

So "B" is twice as likely under AB as under BC, and A (free in AB) keeps probability 2/3.

Solution

Setup. Two of A, B, C will be released: AB, AC or BC, each with probability \(\tfrac13\). The warden names a released prisoner other than A: with AB he says B, with AC he says C, with BC he says B or C with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. He says “B”.

  1. \(P(AB \text{ and “B”}) = \tfrac13\cdot1 = \tfrac13\); \(P(AC \text{ and “B”}) = 0\); \(P(BC \text{ and “B”}) = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac16\).Why: chain rule with the warden's rule.
  2. \(P(\text{“B”}) = \tfrac13 + 0 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac12\).Why: the three release pairs are disjoint and cover everything.
  3. A is released in the cases AB and AC, so \(P(A \text{ released} \mid \text{“B”}) = \dfrac{1/3 + 0}{1/2} = \) \(\tfrac23\).Why: definition of conditional probability.

Conclusion. Hearing “B” leaves A's chance at \(\tfrac23\): it doesn't drop to \(\tfrac12\), because BC produces “B” only half the time. ∎

10 · Reduced sample space

In repeated independent trials, drop the outcomes that change nothing. The subsequence of relevant trials is again independent and identically distributed, with the relevant probabilities renormalised (\(p_i / \sum_{\text{relevant}} p\)). This is the "A before B" lemma from §5 in general form.

Tennis from 30–30

In a tennis game at 30–30 (or at deuce), a player must get two points ahead of the other to win the game. Alice wins each point with probability 0.6, independently of all other points.

Question: starting from 30–30, what is the probability that Alice wins the game?

Picture

Look at points in pairs from a level score. Alice wins both (0.36): she wins. Bob wins both (0.16): he wins. Split (0.48): level again, nothing changed. So only AA vs BB matters: 0.36 ÷ (0.36 + 0.16).

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one pair of points played from a level score. AA → ends (win). BB → ends (lose). AB or BA → level again → next turn.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i) = P(Alice wins the game, looking from turn i onward), where turn i is the i-th pair of points played from a level score. 30–30 is level, so the answer is rec(1).Why: from a level score one point puts someone only 1 ahead, which is not enough, so the game can only end at the end of a pair. That makes a pair of points the natural turn.
  2. CHOICE. At turn i: AA with probability \(0.6 \cdot 0.6 = 0.36\); BB with \(0.4 \cdot 0.4 = 0.16\); AB or BA with \(2 \cdot 0.6 \cdot 0.4 = 0.48\).Why: points are independent; the four orders AA, AB, BA, BB cover everything, and \(0.36 + 0.16 + 0.48 = 1\).

  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i) = 0.36 \cdot 1 + 0.16 \cdot 0 + 0.48\,\text{rec}(i+1)\).Why: law of total probability over turn i.

  6. SOLVE. Let \(x = \text{rec}(i)\). \(x = 0.36 + 0.48x\), so \(0.52x = 0.36\), so \(x = \tfrac{36}{52} = \) \(\tfrac{9}{13} \approx 0.69231\).Why: substitute MEMORYLESS into MOVE; divide top and bottom of \(\tfrac{36}{52}\) by 4.

Two 2s and two 3s before the first 6

A fair die is rolled over and over until the first 6 appears.

Question: what is the probability that, before that first 6, exactly two 2s and exactly two 3s were rolled? (Any number of 1s, 4s and 5s may appear too.)

Picture

Throw away the rolls that change nothing (1, 4, 5). What is left is a fair 3-sided die: 2, 3 or 6. You need exactly two 2s and two 3s in the first four rolls of it, then a 6.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one roll. State (a, b) = 2s and 3s so far. 6 → ends. A third 2 or 3 → ends (fail). 1, 4, 5 → same state (a self-loop).

  1. LEVEL. rec(i, a, b) = P(exactly two 2s and two 3s come before the first 6), given no 6 yet and a 2s and b 3s among rolls 1 to i−1 (a, b ≤ 2). The answer is rec(1, 0, 0).Why: only the counts of 2s and 3s so far affect the event.
  2. CHOICE. At roll i: 1, 4 or 5 (\(\tfrac36 = \tfrac12\)); 2 (\(\tfrac16\)); 3 (\(\tfrac16\)); 6 (\(\tfrac16\)).Why: a fair die.
  3. CHECK. 6: ends, value 1 if a = b = 2, else 0. 2: continue to (i+1, a+1, b), but if a+1 = 3 there are too many 2s: value 0. 3: likewise with b. 1, 4, 5: continue to (i+1, a, b).Why: the event asks for exactly two of each.

  5. MEMORYLESS. rec(i+1, a, b) = rec(i, a, b) = rec(a, b). SOLVE. rec(2,2) = ⅓(0 + 0 + 1) = ⅓. rec(2,1) = rec(1,2) = ⅓(0 + ⅓) = \(\tfrac19\). rec(2,0) = rec(0,2) = ⅓·\(\tfrac19\) = \(\tfrac{1}{27}\). rec(1,1) = ⅓(\(\tfrac19 + \tfrac19\)) = \(\tfrac{2}{27}\). rec(1,0) = rec(0,1) = ⅓(\(\tfrac{1}{27} + \tfrac{2}{27}\)) = \(\tfrac{1}{27}\). rec(0,0) = ⅓(\(\tfrac{1}{27} + \tfrac{1}{27}\)) = \(\tfrac{2}{81} \approx 0.02469\).

11 · Geometric probability

If a point is uniform in a region \(\Omega\), then \(P(A) = \operatorname{vol}(A)/\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)\). Independent uniform variables give a point uniform in a box. Draw the region, then use areas, volumes or integrals.

\(1 \le y/x \le 2\)

\(x\) and \(y\) are chosen independently and uniformly at random from the interval \([0, 1]\).

Question: what is the probability that \(y/x\) lies between 1 and 2, i.e. \(1 \le y/x \le 2\)?

Key idea

The region between the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = 2x\) inside the unit square is a triangle.

Picture

Probability = area. The condition is the wedge between the lines y = x and y = 2x. Inside the unit square that wedge is a triangle of area ¼.

Solution

Setup. \(x, y\) are independent and uniform on \([0, 1]\), so \((x, y)\) is uniform on the unit square and probability equals area.

  1. \(1 \le y/x \le 2 \iff x \le y \le 2x\) (for \(x > 0\); \(x = 0\) has probability 0).Why: multiply the inequalities by \(x > 0\).
  2. Inside the square, this is the region between the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = 2x\). The line \(y = 2x\) leaves the square through the top at \(x = \tfrac12\), so the region is the triangle \((0,0), (\tfrac12, 1), (1, 1)\).Why: \(2x = 1\) at \(x = \tfrac12\); \(y = x\) reaches the top at \((1,1)\).
  3. Its area is \(\tfrac12 \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1 = \tfrac14\): base from \((\tfrac12, 1)\) to \((1, 1)\) along the top edge, height 1 down to \((0,0)\).Why: triangle area formula.
  4. \(P = \) \(\tfrac14\).Why: probability = area.

Conclusion. The probability is \(\tfrac14\). ∎

⚠ Course writes the area as \(\tfrac12\cdot1\cdot1 = \tfrac12\) and then uses \(\tfrac14\). The triangle's base is \(\tfrac12\) and its height is 1, so the area is \(\tfrac14\) (step 3).

Two friends, 30-minute stays

Two friends each arrive at a café at a random time between 12:00 and 13:00 (uniform and independent). Each stays for exactly 30 minutes and then leaves.

Question: what is the probability that the two friends are at the café at the same time?

Key idea

They meet \(\iff |x - y| \le \tfrac12\). The complement is two corner triangles.

Picture

They meet unless one arrives more than half an hour after the other. Those "miss" cases are two corner triangles of area ⅛ each; everything else is a meeting.

Solution

Setup. Arrival times \(x, y\) are independent and uniform on \([0, 1]\) hour; each friend stays for half an hour. The point \((x, y)\) is uniform on the unit square.

  1. They meet exactly when \(|x - y| \le \tfrac12\).Why: say \(x \le y\). The first friend is there during \([x, x + \tfrac12]\); they meet iff the second arrives by then, \(y \le x + \tfrac12\). The case \(y \le x\) is the same with the roles swapped.
  2. The complement \(|x - y| > \tfrac12\) is two corner triangles, \(y > x + \tfrac12\) and \(x > y + \tfrac12\), each a right triangle with legs \(\tfrac12\): area \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac18\) each.Why: for example, \(y > x + \tfrac12\) inside the square is the triangle \((0, \tfrac12), (0, 1), (\tfrac12, 1)\).
  3. \(P(\text{meet}) = 1 - 2\cdot\tfrac18 = \) \(\tfrac34\).Why: complement of the two triangles.

Conclusion. They meet with probability \(\tfrac34\). ∎

\(xy \le \tfrac12\)

\(x\) and \(y\) are chosen independently and uniformly from \([0, 1]\).

Question: what is the probability that \(xy \le \tfrac12\)?

Key idea

For \(x \le \tfrac12\) every \(y\) works. For \(x > \tfrac12\) you need \(y \le \tfrac{1}{2x}\).

Picture

For x up to ½ every y works; beyond that y must stay under the curve y = 1/(2x). Area = ½ + (area under the curve from ½ to 1).

Solution

Setup. \(x, y\) independent uniform on \([0, 1]\); probability = area of \(\{xy \le \tfrac12\}\) in the unit square.

  1. For a fixed \(x\), the allowed \(y\) are \(y \le \tfrac{1}{2x}\).Why: divide \(xy \le \tfrac12\) by \(x > 0\).
  2. If \(x \le \tfrac12\), then \(\tfrac{1}{2x} \ge 1\), so every \(y \in [0, 1]\) works: a vertical strip of length 1. If \(x > \tfrac12\), the allowed \(y\) fill \([0, \tfrac{1}{2x}]\), of length \(\tfrac{1}{2x} < 1\).Why: compare \(\tfrac{1}{2x}\) with 1.
  3. Area \(= \displaystyle\int_0^{1/2} 1\,dx + \int_{1/2}^{1} \frac{dx}{2x} = \frac12 + \frac12\big[\ln x\big]_{1/2}^{1} = \frac12 + \frac12(0 - \ln\tfrac12) = \frac12 + \frac12\ln 2\).Why: add the strip lengths; \(-\ln\tfrac12 = \ln 2\).
  4. \(P = \) \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\ln 2 \approx 0.84657\).Why: probability = area.

Conclusion. \(P(xy \le \tfrac12) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\ln2\). ∎

Sum of \(n\) uniforms \(< 1\)

\(x_1, \dots, x_n\) are chosen independently and uniformly from \([0, 1]\).

Question: what is the probability that \(x_1 + \dots + x_n < 1\) when (a) \(n = 2\), (b) \(n = 3\), (c) \(n\) is general?

Key idea

The region is a simplex of volume \(1/n!\). Slice at \(x_n = t\) and use induction.

Picture

The region "sum below 1" is a corner of the cube: a triangle of area ½ in 2-D, a pyramid of volume ⅙ in 3-D, and 1/n! in n dimensions.

Solution

Setup. \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) are independent uniforms on \([0, 1]\), so \(P(\sum x_i < 1)\) is the volume of \(\{x \in [0,1]^n : \sum x_i < 1\}\). For \(0 \le s \le 1\) define \(V_n(s)\) = the volume of \(\{x \in [0,1]^n : \sum x_i < s\}\).

Claim. \(V_n(s) = \dfrac{s^n}{n!}\), so \(P = \dfrac{1}{n!}\).

  1. \(n = 1\): \(V_1(s) = s\), the length of \([0, s)\).Why: one variable.
  2. For \(s \le 1\) the upper bounds \(x_i \le 1\) never bind: if all \(x_i \ge 0\) and \(\sum x_i < s \le 1\), each \(x_i < 1\).Why: each term is at most the sum.
  3. Slice at \(x_n = t\), for \(0 \le t < s\): the other coordinates must satisfy \(\sum_{i<n} x_i < s - t\), a cross-section of volume \(V_{n-1}(s - t)\). So \(V_n(s) = \int_0^s V_{n-1}(s - t)\,dt\).Why: volume = integral of cross-section areas (Cavalieri).
  4. By induction, \(V_n(s) = \displaystyle\int_0^s \frac{(s-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,dt = \Big[-\frac{(s-t)^n}{n!}\Big]_0^s = \frac{s^n}{n!}\).Why: assume the formula for \(n - 1\), substitute, and integrate.
  5. \(P = V_n(1) = \tfrac{1}{n!}\): (a) \(\tfrac12\), (b) \(\tfrac16 \approx 0.167\), (c) \(\tfrac{1}{n!}\).Why: \(s = 1\).

Conclusion. The sum of \(n\) uniforms is below 1 with probability \(\tfrac{1}{n!}\). ∎

Crawling ant (max 3 steps)

An ant moves in steps.
For each step it draws a random time \(t\) and a random distance \(x\), each uniform on \([0, 1]\), with all draws independent, and walks distance \(x\) in time \(t\).
It stops at the end of the step during which its total time first goes past 1, but it never takes more than 3 steps.

Question: what is the probability that the total distance it walks is greater than 1?

Key idea

Step 1 never stops the ant (\(t_1 < 1\)). So it stops after 2 steps (\(t_1+t_2 > 1\)) or after 3 steps. Distances and times are independent.

Picture

Split on how many steps the ant takes. The first step never uses up the time. It takes 2 steps if t₁ + t₂ > 1 (chance ½), else 3. Distances are independent of times, so multiply: ½ × P(2 distances pass 1) + ½ × P(3 distances pass 1) = ½·½ + ½·⅚.

Solution

Setup. Step \(k\) takes time \(t_k\) and covers distance \(x_k\); all the \(t_k, x_k\) are independent uniforms on \([0, 1]\). The ant stops at the end of the step during which the total time passes 1, or after 3 steps, whichever comes first. Its final position is the total distance walked.

  1. The ant never stops after step 1.Why: \(t_1 < 1\) (with probability 1), so the time hasn't passed 1, and it has taken fewer than 3 steps.
  2. It stops after step 2 exactly when \(t_1 + t_2 > 1\); otherwise it stops after step 3.Why: the time rule, and the 3-step cap.
  3. \(P(\text{stops after 2 and ends past 1}) = P(t_1 + t_2 > 1)\cdot P(x_1 + x_2 > 1) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14\).Why: times are independent of distances; \(P(\text{sum of 2 uniforms} < 1) = \tfrac12\) (previous problem), so \(P(> 1) = \tfrac12\).
  4. \(P(\text{stops after 3 and ends past 1}) = P(t_1 + t_2 \le 1)\cdot P(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 > 1) = \tfrac12\cdot\big(1 - \tfrac16\big) = \tfrac{5}{12}\).Why: same independence; \(P(\text{sum of 3 uniforms} < 1) = \tfrac16\).
  5. The two cases are disjoint, so \(P = \tfrac14 + \tfrac{5}{12} = \tfrac{3}{12} + \tfrac{5}{12} = \) \(\tfrac23 \approx 0.667\).Why: the ant stops after exactly one of 2 or 3 steps (steps 1–2).

Conclusion. The ant ends past 1 with probability \(\tfrac23\) (simulated). ∎

\(a_1 < a_2 < a_3\) with \(a_k \sim U[0,k]\)

\(a_1\) is uniform on \([0, 1]\), \(a_2\) is uniform on \([0, 2]\), and \(a_3\) is uniform on \([0, 3]\), all independent.

Question: what is the probability that \(a_1 < a_2 < a_3\)?

Key idea

Volume of the ordered region inside a \(1\times2\times3\) box.

Picture

The three numbers are a random point in a 1 × 2 × 3 box. The answer is the volume of the part where they are in order, divided by 6. Integrate: for each a₁ < a₂, a₃ has room 3 − a₂.

Solution

Setup. \(a_k\) is uniform on \([0, k]\), independently, so \((a_1, a_2, a_3)\) is uniform on the box \([0,1]\times[0,2]\times[0,3]\) of volume 6. \(P\) = (volume of the ordered part) / 6.

  1. For fixed \(a_1 < a_2\), the allowed \(a_3\) lie in \((a_2, 3]\), of length \(3 - a_2\). So the volume is \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_{a_1}^{2}(3 - a_2)\,da_2\,da_1\).Why: integrate the allowed length over \(a_2\) from \(a_1\) up to 2, then over \(a_1\).
  2. Inner integral: \(\big[3a_2 - \tfrac{a_2^2}{2}\big]_{a_1}^{2} = (6 - 2) - \big(3a_1 - \tfrac{a_1^2}{2}\big) = 4 - 3a_1 + \tfrac{a_1^2}{2}\).Why: antiderivative of \(3 - a_2\).
  3. Outer integral: \(\int_0^1 \big(4 - 3a_1 + \tfrac{a_1^2}{2}\big)da_1 = 4 - \tfrac32 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac{24 - 9 + 1}{6} = \tfrac83\).Why: integrate term by term.
  4. \(P = \dfrac{8/3}{6} = \) \(\tfrac49 \approx 0.444\).Why: divide by the box volume.

Conclusion. The three are in increasing order with probability \(\tfrac49\). ∎

12 · Recursion in probability

Every problem here uses the §5 template: LEVEL rec(i, s) → CHOICE → CHECK → MOVE → MEMORYLESS → SOLVE. Choose the state s as the smallest description that decides the future, and merge states that are symmetric.

First to roll a 6

Two players take turns rolling a fair die. The first player to roll a 6 wins.

Question: what is the probability that the player who rolls first wins?

Picture

After a miss, the other player is in exactly your old position. If the roller wins with x, then x = ⅙ + ⅚(1 − x), so x = 6/11.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) + Form 5 (game) · Turn = one roll. 6 → ends (the roller wins). Anything else → next turn, and the other player rolls.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i) = P(the player who rolls at turn i wins the game), given no 6 in turns 1 to i−1. The answer is rec(1).Why: the first player is the roller at turn 1. rec is about 'the current roller', not a fixed player.
  2. CHOICE. At turn i the roll is a 6 with probability \(\tfrac16\), not a 6 with probability \(\tfrac56\).Why: a fair die.
  3. CHECK. 6: ends, the roller wins, value 1. Not 6: continue to turn i+1, where the other player rolls.Why: the rules; players alternate.
  4. MOVE. After a non-6, the roller at turn i+1 wins with rec(i+1), so the roller at turn i wins with \(1 - \text{rec}(i+1)\). So \(\text{rec}(i) = \tfrac16 \cdot 1 + \tfrac56\big(1 - \text{rec}(i+1)\big)\).Why: this is the Form 5 (game) move: my chance = 1 − (the chance of the player who moves next). Exactly one of the two players wins; law of total probability over turn i.
  5. MEMORYLESS. At turn i+1, the player rolling faces exactly what the roller at turn i faced. So \(\text{rec}(i+1) = \text{rec}(i)\).Why: each turn is independent of earlier turns, has the same probabilities and the same rules, and nothing from earlier turns is carried forward. This works because rec is about 'the current roller'; for a fixed player it would alternate.
  6. SOLVE. Let \(x = \text{rec}(i)\). \(x = \tfrac16 + \tfrac56 - \tfrac56x = 1 - \tfrac56x\). So \(\tfrac{11}{6}x = 1\), so \(x = \) \(\tfrac{6}{11} \approx 0.54545\).Why: substitute MEMORYLESS into MOVE; expand; move the x terms left.

Answer. The first player wins with probability \(\tfrac{6}{11}\). ∎

Strictly first to heads

Several players each toss a fair coin once per round, all at the same time, round after round. A player's 'time' is the round in which they get their first head.

Question: (a) You play with 9 friends. What is the probability that your first head comes strictly earlier than every friend's? (b) What is the probability that Alice's first head comes strictly before Bob's? (c) What is the probability that Alice's first head comes strictly before Bob's, and Bob's strictly before Charlie's?

Picture

Ignore all-tails rounds; they change nothing. Then only the first round with any head matters. (b) Of the 3 outcomes (H,T), (T,H), (H,H), only (H,T) is good: ⅓. (c) Alice must be alone first (1 of 7 non-all-tails outcomes), then Bob before Charlie (⅓): 1/7 × 1/3.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one round (everyone tosses). All tails → next turn. Anything else → ends (or, in (c), hands over to problem (b)).

  1. LEVEL. For each part, rec(i) = P(the event happens, looking from round i onward), given every toss in rounds 1 to i−1 was tails. The answer is rec(1).Why: before round 1 nobody has a head.
  2. (a) CHOICE + CHECK. Round i has \(2^{10} = 1024\) equally likely outcomes. Only you get H (\(\tfrac{1}{1024}\)): ends, value 1. All tails (\(\tfrac{1}{1024}\)): continue. Anything else (\(\tfrac{1022}{1024}\)): some friend got H this round, so they are not later than you: ends, value 0.Why: 10 independent fair coins; 'strictly first' fails as soon as a friend has a head no later than you.
  3. (a) MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i) = \tfrac{1}{1024}\cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{1024}\,\text{rec}(i+1) + \tfrac{1022}{1024}\cdot 0\).Why: law of total probability over round i.
  4. (a) MEMORYLESS. At round i+1 still nobody has a head and the coins are the same, so \(\text{rec}(i+1) = \text{rec}(i)\).Why: each turn is independent of earlier turns, has the same probabilities and the same rules, and nothing from earlier turns is carried forward.
  5. (a) SOLVE. \(x = \tfrac{1}{1024} + \tfrac{1}{1024}x\), so \(\tfrac{1023}{1024}x = \tfrac{1}{1024}\), so \(x = \) \(\tfrac{1}{1023}\).Why: move the x term left; multiply by \(\tfrac{1024}{1023}\).
  6. (b) CHOICE + CHECK. Round i, (Alice, Bob): (H, T) \(\tfrac14\): ends, value 1. (T, T) \(\tfrac14\): continue. (T, H) or (H, H) \(\tfrac12\): Bob first or a tie, value 0.Why: two independent fair coins give 4 equally likely outcomes.
  7. (b) MOVE + MEMORYLESS. \(\text{rec}(i) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14\,\text{rec}(i+1)\), and \(\text{rec}(i+1) = \text{rec}(i)\).Why: total probability over round i; same memorylessness as (a).
  8. (b) SOLVE. \(x = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14x\), so \(\tfrac34x = \tfrac14\), so \(x = \) \(\tfrac13\).Why: move the x term left; multiply by \(\tfrac43\).
  9. (c) CHOICE + CHECK. Round i, (Alice, Bob, Charlie): (H, T, T) \(\tfrac18\): Alice is strictly first; what remains is 'Bob strictly before Charlie' from a fresh round, which is problem (b): value \(\tfrac13\). (T, T, T) \(\tfrac18\): continue. Anything else: Bob or Charlie got H no later than Alice, value 0.Why: three independent fair coins give 8 equally likely outcomes; (b) is memoryless, so its value from any fresh round is \(\tfrac13\).
  10. (c) MOVE + MEMORYLESS. \(\text{rec}(i) = \tfrac18\cdot\tfrac13 + \tfrac18\,\text{rec}(i+1)\), and \(\text{rec}(i+1) = \text{rec}(i)\).Why: total probability over round i; same memorylessness.
  11. (c) SOLVE. \(x = \tfrac{1}{24} + \tfrac18x\), so \(\tfrac78x = \tfrac{1}{24}\), so \(x = \tfrac{1}{24}\cdot\tfrac87 = \tfrac{8}{168} = \) \(\tfrac{1}{21} \approx 0.04762\).Why: move the x term left; multiply by \(\tfrac87\).

Answer. \(\tfrac{1}{1023}\), \(\tfrac13\) and \(\tfrac{1}{21}\). ∎

HH ends on an even toss

A fair coin is tossed until two heads in a row appear.

Question: what is the probability that this happens on an even-numbered toss (toss 2, 4, 6, …)?

Picture

After a T you start afresh, but the toss count has moved on by 1 (or 2 after HT). Moving by 1 flips "even" and "odd"; moving by 2 doesn't. That gives x = ½(1 − x) + ¼x + ¼, so x = 3/5.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = a 'fresh start' before toss i (no toss yet, or the last toss was T). T or HT → fresh again. HH → ends. The MEMORYLESS check fails here, and that is the lesson.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i) = P(the first HH is completed on an even toss number), given we are fresh just before toss i. The answer is rec(1).Why: before toss 1 nothing has been tossed, so we are fresh.
  2. CHOICE. From fresh before toss i: toss i is T (\(\tfrac12\)); or toss i is H and toss i+1 is T (\(\tfrac14\)); or tosses i, i+1 are HH (\(\tfrac14\)).Why: toss i is T, or it is H and then toss i+1 is T or H. Disjoint, and \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1\).
  3. CHECK. HH: ends on toss i+1, which is even exactly when i is odd. T: fresh before toss i+1. HT: fresh before toss i+2.Why: a T can never be part of a later HH, so after a T nothing carries over.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i) = \tfrac12\,\text{rec}(i+1) + \tfrac14\,\text{rec}(i+2) + \tfrac14\cdot[i \text{ odd}]\), where \([i \text{ odd}]\) is 1 if i is odd and 0 otherwise.Why: law of total probability over the three cases.
  5. MEMORYLESS (check it, don't assume it). Let M = the number of tosses a brand-new game takes.
    Fresh before toss j, the rest is a brand-new game, so it ends at toss \(j - 1 + M\): \(\text{rec}(j) = P(j - 1 + M \text{ even})\). So:\[\begin{aligned}\text{rec}(i+1) &= P(i + M \text{ even}) = 1 - P(i - 1 + M \text{ even}) = 1 - \text{rec}(i),\\ \text{rec}(i+2) &= P(i + 1 + M \text{ even}) = P(i - 1 + M \text{ even}) = \text{rec}(i).\end{aligned}\]Why: the coin forgets, but the question is about the toss number: its parity flips when the start moves by 1 and stays when it moves by 2. So here rec(i+1) ≠ rec(i).
  6. SOLVE. Take i = 1 (odd), \(x = \text{rec}(1)\). \(x = \tfrac12(1 - x) + \tfrac14x + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12x + \tfrac14x + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34 - \tfrac14x\). So \(\tfrac54x = \tfrac34\), so \(x = \) \(\tfrac35\).Why: substitute MEMORYLESS into MOVE; collect x terms; multiply by \(\tfrac45\). (Simulated: 0.600.)

Answer. The first HH ends on an even toss with probability \(\tfrac35\). ∎

Parcel around 5 seats

5 people sit around a round table; you are one of them, and you are holding a parcel.
On each turn, whoever holds the parcel does one of three things, each with probability \(\tfrac13\): passes it left, passes it right, or keeps it.
As soon as someone keeps it, the game ends and that person gets the parcel.

Question: what is the probability that you end up with the parcel?

Picture

Only the distance from you matters: 0, 1 or 2 seats away. That leaves 3 unknowns, one equation each (keep, pass towards, pass away). Check: your share plus the others' must add to 1.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one turn of the holder. State d = holder's distance from you (0, 1 or 2). Keep → ends. Pass → next turn at the new distance.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i, d) = P(you end up keeping the parcel), given that before turn i the holder is at distance d from you. Around the circle the seats are at distances 0 (you), 1, 2, 2, 1. The answer is rec(1, 0).Why: by symmetry only the distance matters.
  2. CHOICE. Keep, pass left, pass right: each \(\tfrac13\).Why: the rules.
  3. CHECK. Keep: ends, value 1 if d = 0, else 0. From d = 0 either pass goes to distance 1. From d = 1: toward you → 0, away → 2. From d = 2: toward you → 1, the other way → the other distance-2 seat, still 2.Why: read off the circle; the two distance-2 seats are next to each other.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i,0) = \tfrac13 + \tfrac23\,\text{rec}(i{+}1,1)\). \(\text{rec}(i,1) = \tfrac13\cdot 0 + \tfrac13\,\text{rec}(i{+}1,0) + \tfrac13\,\text{rec}(i{+}1,2)\). \(\text{rec}(i,2) = \tfrac13\cdot 0 + \tfrac13\,\text{rec}(i{+}1,1) + \tfrac13\,\text{rec}(i{+}1,2)\).Why: law of total probability over the holder's action.
  5. MEMORYLESS. rec(i+1, d) = rec(i, d) = rec(d).Why: the rules at a turn depend only on the state s, never on the turn number i, and the coin/die/walk has no memory. So the future from (i+1, s) is exactly the future from (i, s).

Into rec(0) = ⅓ + ⅔rec(1) = ⅓ + \(\tfrac{4}{15}\)rec(0): \(\tfrac{11}{15}\)rec(0) = ⅓, so rec(0) = \(\tfrac{5}{11} \approx 0.45455\).Why: solve from the far side inwards.
  7. CHECK THE ANSWER. rec(1) = \(\tfrac{2}{11}\), rec(2) = \(\tfrac{1}{11}\), and \(\tfrac{5}{11} + 2\cdot\tfrac{2}{11} + 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{11} = 1\) ✓.Why: by symmetry these are also the chances that each seat ends up keeping it when you start, and somebody always keeps it.

Answer. You keep the parcel with probability \(\tfrac{5}{11}\). ∎

HHT before HTT

A fair coin is tossed over and over until either the pattern HHT or the pattern HTT appears (three tosses in a row).

Question: what is the probability that HHT appears before HTT?

Picture

Once you have HH, HHT is guaranteed: more H's keep you at HH, and the first T finishes HHT. HTT needs H, T, T in a row without another H getting in the way. Track the useful ending: ∅, H, HH, HT.

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one toss. State s = the useful ending so far: ∅, H, HH or HT. HHT → ends (win). HTT → ends (lose).

  1. LEVEL. rec(i, s) = P(HHT comes before HTT), given that before toss i the useful ending is s: the longest ending of the tosses so far that is still the start of a pattern (∅, H, HH or HT). The answer is rec(1, ∅).Why: only the useful ending can become part of a pattern.
  2. CHOICE. Toss i is H or T, each \(\tfrac12\).Why: a fair coin.
  3. CHECK. ∅: H → H, T → ∅. H: H → HH, T → HT. HH: H → HH, T → HHT, ends, value 1. HT: H → H, T → HTT, ends, value 0.Why: after HTH the useful ending is just H; after HHH it is still HH.

  1. If the process treats labels interchangeably, every label (pair, ordering, …) has the same probability. If exactly one of \(k\) such cases happens, each has probability \(1/k\).

Key idea

On any fixed move, every one of the \(\binom{100}{2}\) label pairs is equally likely to be the pair drawn.

Setup. 100 marbles labelled 1–100.

(a) \(P(\text{marbles 3 and 98 are the pair drawn on move 50})\).
(b) \(P(\text{they are drawn together on some move})\).

Picture

Switches alternate: HT, TH, HT, … So the two counts differ by at most 1, and the difference is decided by the first and last tosses alone. More HT means: starts with H and ends with T.

Solution

Setup. 10 fair tosses. #HT = the number of positions \(i\) where toss \(i\) is H and toss \(i+1\) is T; #TH likewise.

  2. The switches alternate between HT and TH.Why: after an HT switch you are in a T-run, so the next switch must be TH, and vice versa.

19 · PDF and CDF (continuous)

  1. \(X\) has density \(f \ge 0\) if \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f\) for all \(a \le b\). Then \(\int f = 1\), \(P(X = x) = 0\) for every \(x\), and \(f\) can be larger than 1.
  2. \(F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f\), and \(F' = f\) wherever \(f\) is continuous.
  3. To find a density, find the CDF first, then differentiate. This is usually easier than the "\(f(x)\,dx = P(x \le X \le x+dx)\)" method.

Integrate a density

A continuous random variable with density \(f\) has \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).

Question: (a) \(X\) has density \(f(x) = \tfrac38x^2\) for \(0 \le x \le 2\) (and 0 elsewhere). Find \(P(0.5 \le X \le 1.5)\). (b) \(X\) has density \(f(x) = 3x^2\) for \(0 \le x \le 1\). Find its CDF \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\).

Key idea

The area under \(f\).

Picture

Probability = area under the density. Shade the part between the two limits and integrate.

Solution

  1. (a) \(f(x) = \tfrac38x^2\) on \([0, 2]\) is a density: \(\int_0^2 \tfrac38x^2\,dx = \tfrac{x^3}{8}\big|_0^2 = 1\).Why: it is non-negative and integrates to 1.
  2. \(P(.5 \le X \le 1.5) = \int_{.5}^{1.5}\tfrac38x^2\,dx = \dfrac{1.5^3 - .5^3}{8} = \dfrac{3.375 - .125}{8} = \) \(0.40625\).Why: the probability of an interval is the area under the density.
  3. (b) \(f = 3x^2\) on \([0,1]\): \(F(x) = \int_0^x 3t^2\,dt = x^3\) for \(0 \le x \le 1\); \(F = 0\) for \(x < 0\) and \(F = 1\) for \(x > 1\).Why: \(F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f\), and \(f = 0\) outside \([0, 1]\).

Conclusion. 0.40625, and \(F(x) = x^3\) on \([0,1]\). ∎

\(\min > x \iff\) all ten are \(> x\).

21 · The normal distribution

  1. \(\varphi(x) = \dfrac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\) and \(\Phi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} \varphi\).
  2. \(\Phi(x) + \Phi(-x) = 1\). Since \(\varphi\) is even, \(\Phi(-x) = \int_{x}^{\infty} \varphi = 1 - \Phi(x)\).
  3. If \(X \sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) and \(a \ne 0\), then \(aX + b \sim N(a\mu+b,\, a^2\sigma^2)\). So \(Z = (X-\mu)/\sigma \sim N(0,1)\).

Rain and humidity

Question: what is the probability that it rains?

Key idea

Average the conditional probability over the density.

Picture

Average the rain chance over all humidities, weighted by how likely each humidity is: ∫ h² · 2h dh = ½.

Solution

Setup. Humidity \(H\) has density \(2h\) on \([0, 1]\), and \(P(\text{rain} \mid H = h) = h^2\).

  1. \(P(\text{rain}) = \int_0^1 P(\text{rain} \mid H = h)\,f_H(h)\,dh = \int_0^1 h^2\cdot2h\,dh\).Why: continuous law of total probability.
  2. \(= \int_0^1 2h^3\,dh = \tfrac{2}{4} = \) \(\tfrac12\).Why: \(\int_0^1 h^3\,dh = \tfrac14\).

Conclusion. It rains with probability \(\tfrac12\). ∎

Nested points in the disc

A first point is placed in the unit disc so that its distance \(R_1\) from the centre is uniform on \([0, 1]\). Then a second point is placed uniformly at random (by area) in the ring between radius \(R_1\) and radius 1.

Question: what is the probability that the second point is more than \(\tfrac12\) away from the centre?

Key idea

Given \(r_1\), \(P(R_2 > \tfrac12)\) is an area ratio. Integrate it over \(r_1\).

Picture

Condition on where the first point lands. If it is beyond ½, the second point surely is too. If not, the chance is an area ratio: (ring from ½ to 1) ÷ (ring from r₁ to 1). Average over r₁.

Solution

Setup. The first point's distance from the centre is \(R_1 \sim U[0, 1]\). Given \(R_1 = r_1\), the second point is uniform (by area) in the annulus \(r_1 < r \le 1\). We want \(P(R_2 > \tfrac12)\).

  1. If \(r_1 \ge \tfrac12\): every point of the annulus is at distance \(> r_1 \ge \tfrac12\), so \(P(R_2 > \tfrac12 \mid r_1) = 1\).Why: the annulus starts beyond \(\tfrac12\).
  2. If \(r_1 < \tfrac12\): the favourable part is the ring from \(\tfrac12\) to 1, of area \(\pi(1 - \tfrac14)\), out of the annulus area \(\pi(1 - r_1^2)\). So \(P(R_2 > \tfrac12 \mid r_1) = \dfrac{3/4}{1 - r_1^2}\).Why: uniform by area.
  3. \(P = \displaystyle\int_0^{1/2}\frac{3/4}{1 - r^2}\,dr + \int_{1/2}^{1}1\,dr\).Why: continuous total probability over \(R_1\), whose density is 1 on \([0,1]\).
  4. \(\displaystyle\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dr}{1 - r^2} = \frac12\ln\frac{1 + r}{1 - r}\Big|_0^{1/2} = \frac12\ln 3\).Why: partial fractions: \(\tfrac{1}{1-r^2} = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac{1}{1-r} + \tfrac{1}{1+r}\big)\); at \(r = \tfrac12\), \(\tfrac{1.5}{0.5} = 3\).
  5. \(P = \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac12\ln3 + \tfrac12 = \dfrac{4 + 3\ln 3}{8} \approx\) \(0.91198\).Why: substitute step 4 into step 3.

Question: what is the probability that every two of them are at least \(90^\circ\) apart, measured the short way round the circle?

Picture

Solution

  2. \(P_2\) must lie in \([90^\circ, 270^\circ]\). 24 · Law of total expectation

\(\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_y \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\,P(Y = y)\), or \(\int \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\,f_Y(y)\,dy\). Compactly, the tower property: \(\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big] = \mathbb{E}[X]\).

Shift the coordinates to \(-4..4\) (area is unchanged) so every coordinate has mean 0. Then only the 6 squared terms survive.

Picture

Solution

Question: what is the expected sum of all the rolls, including the final 6?

Picture

Solution

Form 1 (i → end) · Turn = one roll. 6 → adds 6 and ends. 1–5 → adds that value, next turn.

  1. LEVEL. rec(i) = the expected sum of the rolls from turn i onward (turn i included), given no 6 in turns 1 to i−1. The answer is rec(1).Why: 'from turn i onward' is the amount still to come.
  2. CHOICE. At turn i the roll is k, for k = 1, …, 6, each with probability \(\tfrac16\).Why: a fair die.
  3. CHECK. k = 6: adds 6, ends. k ≤ 5: adds k, continue to turn i+1.Why: the rules.
  4. MOVE. \(\text{rec}(i) = \tfrac16\cdot 6 + \sum_{k=1}^{5}\tfrac16\big(k + \text{rec}(i+1)\big)\).Why: law of total expectation over turn i: a continuing outcome contributes prob × (this roll's value + what is still to come).
  5. MEMORYLESS. At turn i+1 the roll is again uniform on 1–6 with the same stopping rule, so \(\text{rec}(i+1) = \text{rec}(i)\).Why: each turn is independent of earlier turns, has the same probabilities and the same rules, and nothing from earlier turns is carried forward.

If a transformation \(T\) keeps the law of the randomness unchanged and sends \(X\) to \(c - X\) (or \(-X\)), then \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[c - X]\), so \(\mathbb{E}[X] = c/2\) (or 0). For random walks, pair the moves \(m\) and \(-m\): \(\mathbb{E}|v + m|^2 = |v|^2 + \mathbb{E}|m|^2\).

Mixture of two normals

With probability \(h\), \(Y\) is drawn from a normal distribution with mean \(\mu_h\) and variance \(\sigma_h^2\); otherwise (probability \(1 - h\)) it is drawn from a normal distribution with mean \(\mu_t\) and variance \(\sigma_t^2\).

Question: find the variance of \(Y\).

Key idea

Within-group variance + between-group variance.

Picture

Spread inside each group + spread between the groups. Within: h σh² + (1 − h) σt². Between: the two centres, weighted h and 1 − h.

Solution

Setup. Let \(I = 1\) with probability \(h\) and \(I = 0\) otherwise. Given \(I = 1\), \(Y \sim N(\mu_h, \sigma_h^2)\); given \(I = 0\), \(Y \sim N(\mu_t, \sigma_t^2)\).

  1. \(\operatorname{Var}Y = \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid I)] + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid I])\).Why: law of total variance.
  2. \(\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid I)] = h\sigma_h^2 + (1-h)\sigma_t^2\).Why: the conditional variance is \(\sigma_h^2\) or \(\sigma_t^2\), weighted by the probabilities.
  3. \(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid I]\) equals \(\mu_h\) with probability \(h\) and \(\mu_t\) otherwise; its variance is \(h(1-h)(\mu_h - \mu_t)^2\).Why: for a two-point variable with values \(a\) (prob \(h\)) and \(b\): the mean is \(m = ha + (1-h)b\), \(a - m = (1-h)(a-b)\), \(b - m = -h(a-b)\), so the variance is \(h(1-h)^2(a-b)^2 + (1-h)h^2(a-b)^2 = h(1-h)(a-b)^2\).
  4. \(\operatorname{Var}Y = h\sigma_h^2 + (1-h)\sigma_t^2 + h(1-h)(\mu_h - \mu_t)^2\).Why: add steps 2 and 3.

Conclusion. Variance = within-group + between-group. ∎
